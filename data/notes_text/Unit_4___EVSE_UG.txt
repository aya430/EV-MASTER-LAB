Unit 4: EV Modelling and Simulation
4.1     Introduction to EV Modelling and Simulation
4.1.1    Introduction to EV System Modelling
An electric vehicle is a coupled system. Electrical energy leaves the battery, passes through an
inverter and motor, turns into torque at the wheels and finally appears as motion, aerodynamic
heat, tyre heat and a small amount of stored kinetic and potential energy. A change anywhere in
this chain affects everything else. Heavier batteries add range but also add road load; a higher
gear ratio improves launch torque but raises motor speed at cruise and may push the motor into
a less efficient region. A model is a set of equations and data that captures these interactions,
and a simulation is the numerical solution of that model for a chosen scenario [1, 2].
Units 2 and 3 described the battery, power converters and machines individually. This unit joins
them into complete vehicle models, and uses those models to answer questions such as: how
much energy does a car need on a test cycle, how much of the braking energy can be recovered,
what size of motor and battery will meet a specification, and how far can the car travel?

4.1.2    Importance of EV Modelling and Simulation
Prototype testing is slow and expensive, and a chassis dynamometer can only test hardware that
already exists. Simulation is valuable for these reasons:

   • Cost and time. Thousands of battery, motor and gear combinations can be screened in
     the time it takes to build one prototype.

   • Early decisions. The most expensive choices, such as pack voltage, cell format and drive
     layout, are made before hardware exists.

   • Safe testing. Faults, thermal runaway precursors and extreme braking can be explored
     without risk to people or equipment.

   • Repeatability. A simulated test is exactly repeatable, so the effect of one parameter can
     be isolated.

   • Control development. Algorithms can be tested against a virtual vehicle before they are
     loaded on an ECU.

4.1.3    Objectives of EV Simulation
A simulation study always starts with a stated objective, because that objective decides which
model is adequate. Typical objectives are to predict energy consumption and range over a


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                              15
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                    B24EES502


drive cycle; to size the battery, motor and inverter; to evaluate acceleration, top speed and
gradeability; to quantify regenerative energy recovery; to develop and tune control strategies;
and to estimate component stresses such as peak current, temperature and duty.

4.1.4    Modelling Terms: Definitions and Differences
Six terms are often confused. Their meanings are distinct, and Table 4.1 summarises them.

   1. Mathematical modelling expresses system behaviour as equations, for example δmv̇ =
      Ft − Fres . It is an abstraction: the equations hold only under stated assumptions.

   2. Physical modelling builds a model by connecting components that exchange energy,
      such as a battery connected to an inverter through electrical ports. The tool writes the
      equations from the topology. Simscape is a common example [34].

   3. Simulation is the numerical solution of a model over time for specified inputs and initial
      conditions.

   4. System identification estimates model parameters from measured data, for example fit-
      ting R0 , R1 and C1 to a current pulse test (Unit 2) or fitting road-load coefficients to a
      coast-down test.

   5. Model verification asks: did we solve the equations correctly? It checks code, units,
      solver accuracy and energy balance.

   6. Model validation asks: did we build the right model? It compares predictions with
      independent measurements over the intended operating range.

4.1.6    Simulation-Based Design and the Simulation Workflow
Simulation-based design uses models as the main design tool, with hardware tests reserved
for confirmation. Figure 4.2 shows the workflow. The objective is defined first, parameters are
collected, the model is built and its code verified. It is then validated against data. A mismatch
sends the work back to parameter identification, rather than forward to conclusions. Scenarios
are run only after that, and the analysis either refines the design (returning to the model) or
releases it for prototype test. The loops in the figure are the essential point: a simulation is a
process of iteration, not a single calculation.

More detail is not automatically better. Model fidelity should match the objective, since each

4.1.8    System-Level, Component-Level and Vehicle-Level Modelling
Figure 4.3 shows the modelling hierarchy. Component-level models describe one device in
detail, such as a cell or a machine. System-level models join these devices into subsystems such
as the powertrain, thermal loop or brake system. A vehicle-level model joins all subsystems to
predict quantities only the whole vehicle has, such as range and 0–100 km/h time. The vertical
arrows carry parameters upward (for example, a cell’s resistance becoming a pack resistance)
and demands downward (for example, a torque request becoming a current in each cell). Good
practice is to validate each level separately before validating the assembled vehicle.

4.1.14     Integrated EV Simulation Platforms
An integrated platform connects several domains in one environment: electrical, mechanical,

   • Vehicle design. Trade-offs between mass, drag and rolling resistance can be expressed

   • Battery sizing. The cycle consumption and range target fix usable energy; peak power

   • Energy consumption prediction. This is the main output of a drive-cycle simulation

   • Range estimation. Consumption and usable energy together give range (Section 4.15).

   • Regenerative braking analysis. Simulation shows how much braking energy reaches
     the battery after limits and losses (Section 4.6).

   • Control strategy development. Controllers are tuned against a virtual vehicle (Sec-

   • Performance optimisation. Parameters are swept, or optimised, against a cost such as

4.2     Modelling of BEV: Forward-Looking Model
4.2.1    Introduction to Forward-Looking Modelling
A forward-looking model simulates a vehicle in the same order that cause follows effect in
a real car. A driver sees a target speed and moves the pedals. The pedals produce a torque
command. The motor produces torque, the torque accelerates the vehicle and the resulting
speed is compared with the target. The approach is also called a driver-oriented or causal
model [2, 11].
The approach is widely used in EV engineering texts [4, 6]. Its defining features are:

   • the input is a target, not the actual speed;

   • the actual speed is an output obtained by integrating the equation of motion;

   • a driver model is needed to close the loop;

   • component limits act naturally, so the vehicle can fail to follow the target.

4.3      Backward-Looking Model: Drive Cycle Perspective
4.3.1    Introduction to Backward-Looking Models
A backward-looking model reverses the logic. It assumes that the vehicle follows the pre-
scribed speed exactly. Starting from that speed, it calculates the force required at the wheels,
then the torque and speed at the motor, then the electrical power at the inverter and the battery.

School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                        30
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                                   B24EES502


Calculations run from the road back to the energy source, hence the name [2, 11]. The method
is also called the drive-cycle or quasi-static approach, since each sample is treated as a steady
operating point.
Its key features are:

   • the speed profile is an input; there is no driver model;

   • no differential equation for speed is solved, so there is no speed feedback;

   • the only integrated quantities are energy and SoC;

   • demand is assumed to be met, whether or not the hardware can deliver it.

4.3.12     Assumptions and Limitations
The method assumes that the vehicle follows the trace perfectly, that each sample is quasi-static,
and that the road and wheels do not slip. Its limitations follow directly:

   • It cannot show that a car fails to follow a cycle; torque above the motor limit is simply
     computed. An output check must flag such samples.

   • Driver and control dynamics are absent, so it cannot be used for tuning controllers or for
     hardware-in-the-loop tests.

   • Acceleration from numerical differences depends on sample rate; 1 Hz data hide short
     peaks.

   • Thermal and other transient limits must be handled outside the main calculation.

4.4.2      Why Each Is Useful
Backward models for energy studies. Energy depends on the speed trace and the efficiency
chain, not on the fine detail of a controller. Since the backward model follows the trace exactly,
every candidate design is compared on the same work done at the wheels, and thousands of
runs are affordable. For the reference BEV on MIDC, the backward net energy is 1.302 kWh
and the forward PI-driver result is 1.289 kWh, a difference of 1%. Tracking the cycle to within
about 0.3 km/h rms therefore has little effect on energy.
Forward models for control and actual response. Only a causal model has a vehicle that can
fall behind its target. If a controller limits torque, a forward model shows the speed falling, the
driver pressing harder and the integrator winding up. Pedal mapping, jerk limits, regenerative
blending and driveability cannot be studied any other way.

4.4.3      Combined and Hybrid Modelling Approaches
ADVISOR combined the two ideas [11, 12]. A backward pass computes what is demanded,
a check finds what the components can supply, and a forward pass returns what was actually
delivered, as in Figure 4.22. If the limit is not reached, the vehicle follows the trace, retaining
the backward model’s speed. If it is reached, the delivered speed replaces the demand, and the
trace miss is reported. Other hybrids use a fast backward model for design optimisation and a
forward model for final verification, which is the common industrial practice. Gao et al. review
these structures in [1].

4.5       Modelling of Driver
4.5.1      Introduction and Role of the Driver in EV Simulation
In a forward-looking simulation, the driver model is the block that makes the vehicle follow
a speed target. It stands in for the human, or for the test technician who follows a trace on a
dynamometer. Its job is not to imitate a person perfectly, but to generate realistic, repeatable
pedal commands that keep the vehicle within an allowed band around the reference. The driver
matters in three ways: it closes the speed loop, it sets how aggressively the powertrain is used,
and it therefore influences energy consumption and component stress.

4.6.2    Conventional Friction Braking
In a friction brake, hydraulic pressure clamps pads onto a disc. The brake torque at one wheel

4.6.5    Brake Control Unit and EV Braking Architecture

4.6.10     Limits on Regenerative Braking
Regenerative torque is available only within several limits, each of which may be the active
one.

   • Motor torque limit. |T m | ≤ T pk (ωm ) on the generator side of the envelope.

   • Inverter current limit. Phase current cannot exceed the device rating; this acts through
     the same torque limit.

   • Battery charge acceptance. Charge power is limited to Pchg (z, Θ), which falls in a
     high SoC and at low temperature. Lithium plating risk makes cold charging particularly
     restricted.

   • Battery SoC limit. Above about 95% SoC regeneration is largely unavailable, for ex-
     ample just after a full charge.

   • Low-speed limit. Back EMF and power both vanish as ωm → 0, so regeneration fades
     in below about 5–15 km/h. Friction takes over for the final stop, or the inverter briefly
     holds torque while drawing a small battery current.

    • Fault conditions. Over-voltage on the DC link and sensor faults disable regeneration.

4.6.12     Brake Force Distribution, Front and Rear Axle Braking
Deceleration moves load to the front axle. With wheelbase L, CG height hcg and distances l f
and lr from the CG to the front and rear axles, axle loads in N for deceleration ad are
                                (︄         )︄                 (︄          )︄
                            mg         ad                 mg          ad
                     Fz f =        lr + hcg ,      Fzr =         l f − hcg .            (4.52)
                             L         g                   L          g

The ideal distribution divides the brake force in proportion to these loads, so that both axles
reach the adhesion limit together. In practice the distribution is kept front-biased, since locking
the rear wheels first makes the car unstable. A single front motor can regenerate only through
the front axle, and a rear motor only through the rear, so the rear share must stay within stability
limits. For a drive axle share β, the regenerative torque cannot exceed βT b∗ without deviating
from the intended distribution unless the system permits a safe bias.

4.6.13     ABS Interaction and Brake-by-Wire Systems
Under hard braking on a slippery road, ABS modulates pressure at each wheel to hold longitu-
dinal slip near the peak of the friction curve. Regenerative torque acts through a gearbox and
has a different dynamic response, so it is usually faded out quickly when ABS activates, leaving
control to the hydraulic system. In some designs, the fast motor torque assists the wheel slip
control.
Brake-by-wire decouples pedal from hydraulics: a sensor reads the pedal, a simulator provides
feel, and an electric pump or booster generates pressure. This is what allows the invisible
exchange of friction for regenerative torque. In fault conditions, a hydraulic fallback connects
the pedal directly to the wheels.

4.6.14     Regenerative Braking under Different Operating Conditions
   • Urban stop-and-go. Frequent mild stops suit regeneration very well; a large share of
     traction energy can be recovered.

   • Highway. Few stops mean drag does the braking, so little energy is available.

   • Long downhill. Power is available for a long time, so thermal limits and a full battery
     may end regeneration. Friction brakes must be able to take over.

   • Cold battery. Charge acceptance is low until the pack warms.

   • Full battery. Regeneration is reduced or absent just after charging.

   • Emergency braking. Friction dominates; the tyres set the limit.

4.7.1     Introduction to EV Vehicle Control
The vehicle control strategy is the logic that decides, at each moment, what torque the power-
train should deliver. It sits between human intention and the machines. A good strategy gives a
car that responds predictably, protects the hardware, uses energy well and behaves safely when
something fails.

4.7.6    Powertrain Operating Modes
Figure 4.50 shows a typical state machine, which defines the following modes:

   • Standby. Contactors are open. Key-on and successful self-checks lead to Ready.

   • Standstill (Ready). High voltage is live with T m∗ = 0, or creep torque in D.

   • Motoring. Forward traction with T m∗ > 0.

   • Regenerative braking. Forward motion with T m∗ < 0, entered by a brake press or lift-off.

   • Reverse. Selected at v ≈ 0. Torque sign reversed and speed limited to a low value.

   • Fault/limp. Torque is reduced or removed, according to severity.

Each arc in the figure carries its guard condition. Transitions between Forward and Reverse
require v ≈ 0, because a direct change of sign at speed would shock the driveline. This makes
the state machine a safety mechanism as well as a sequencer.

4.7.7    Traction Control Overview
Traction control limits wheel slip when torque exceeds what the tyres can transmit. Slip during
traction is λ = (ωw rw − v)/(ωw rw ). If λ exceeds about 0.1–0.15, past the peak of the tyre curve,
then the VCU reduces torque. An electric motor is much better at this than an engine, because
torque can be reduced within a few milliseconds.

4.7.8    Protection and Fault Conditions
The VCU reacts to over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current, over-temperature, insulation
faults, sensor disagreement and CAN timeouts. Reactions are graded: a warning, a derate, limp
mode or an immediate torque cut with contactors opening. The accelerator signal typically
uses two redundant sensors, and a plausibility check forces T m∗ = 0 if they disagree.

4.7.9    Energy Management and Strategy Selection
Energy management in a BEV has fewer degrees of freedom than in a hybrid, because there is
only one energy source, but several choices remain: torque split in a dual-motor car, regenera-

choice of control strategy depends on available computing power, safety requirements and the

4.8.14    Vehicle Dynamics Assumptions
The longitudinal model in this unit assumes that the car is a rigid point mass, that the road and
tyre conditions are uniform, that there is no lateral motion and that suspension pitch can be
neglected. Rolling and drag coefficients are taken as constant, and tyre slip is neglected. These
assumptions are valid for energy prediction, and are poor for handling or limit braking.

4.8.16    Influence of Vehicle Parameters on EV Performance
   • Vehicle mass. Raises rolling, grade and inertial forces in proportion. Regeneration re-
     turns part of the inertial energy, but rolling loss is lost. A 10% mass increase raises urban
     consumption substantially and has less effect on the highway.


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                               75
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                     B24EES502


   • Frontal area and drag coefficient. The product Cd A sets highway consumption; power
     rises with v3 . A 10% reduction in Cd A gives a 10% lower drag power at all speeds.

   • Wheel radius. A larger radius lowers force for a given wheel torque and lowers motor
     speed for a given road speed. It is therefore equivalent to a change in G/rw , and must be
     considered together with the gear ratio.

   • Rolling resistance. Important at low and moderate speeds, and Pr rises only linearly
     with speed. Tyre choice is one of the cheapest ways to gain range.

4.9.1    Introduction and Purpose of Component Sizing
Component sizing selects battery, inverter, motor and gear ratings so that a vehicle meets its tar-
gets at the lowest cost and mass. The sizing is iterative, since a bigger battery adds mass, which
raises road load, which demands more power and energy. A steady-state energy balance is the
natural first step: it shows how much power each component must handle at a defined operating
point, with no integration required. The peak values are then treated in Section 4.11.

4.9.4    Motor, Inverter and Transmission Efficiency, Auxiliary Power
Component efficiency depends on the operating point. A motor efficiency map has its highest
values at moderate torque and mid speeds. A gear pair is highly efficient, typically 0.95–
0.98. An inverter normally exceeds 0.96 at moderate load, with efficiency falling at very low
load since switching and gate-drive losses remain. Auxiliaries include the DC-DC converter,
cooling pumps, and particularly HVAC, which can draw from about 0.5 kW to several kW.
Unlike road load, auxiliary power is independent of speed, so its energy per km rises as speed
falls.

4.9.7    Operating Cases
   • Level-road cruising. θ = 0, a = 0: Pw = Pr + Pa . For the reference car at 90 km/h,
     Pw = 10.6 kW.

   • Hill climbing. Pg = mgv sin θ adds to demand, and dominates on steep roads.

   • Constant-speed driving. This is the general steady case with a = 0 and any grade.

   • Acceleration. Pacc = δmav rises with speed for a constant a, so peak power occurs at the
     end of the acceleration. This case is not steady, but the same power chain applies.

   • Deceleration. Pacc < 0. If |Pacc | < Pr + Pa , the car simply coasts down and no braking is
     needed.

   • Regenerative braking. When Pw < 0, (4.88) applies. A long descent is the steady
     version, in which Pg < 0 can power the battery.


4.9.8    Powertrain Component Selection, Constraints and Safety Margins
Component selection must satisfy limits on current, voltage, temperature and mechanical speed.
A design margin is added because efficiency estimates are uncertain, batteries age, ambient
conditions vary and parts vary between batches. Typical margins are 10–20% on power and
10–15% on energy, and larger on current where semiconductor junction temperature is critical.

4.10.11     Parameter Identification and Validation
Parameters are identified from pulse tests: the instantaneous step gives R0 , and the following

from a drive cycle, comparing simulated and measured Vt with (4.3). A cell model with an
RMSE of a few tens of millivolts is generally acceptable for a vehicle simulation.

4.11.11       Short-Duration Overload and Component Safety Margin
Overload capability differs among components. A motor can tolerate 30 s or more due to its
large thermal mass. An inverter’s capability is limited to seconds. A battery’s peak is commonly
specified as a 10 s or 30 s pulse. The overall peak rating is set by the shortest of these, and the
BMS or inverter counts down remaining overload time. Safety margins should be added to the
final selected rating, not to each intermediate step, or the design will be needlessly oversized.

4.11.12       Powertrain Efficiency
Over a cycle, the average efficiency is the ratio of energies, not the product of constant values.
Efficiency also depends on sizing: an oversized motor runs at a smaller fraction of rated torque
in normal driving, where efficiency tends to be lower. This is a further reason not to oversize.

4.11.15     Selection Based on Peak Power versus Continuous Power
Selecting a motor from peak power ensures acceleration, launch and overtaking, and it leads
to a machine and inverter with a high current rating. Selecting from continuous power ensures
sustained speed, hill climbing and repeatable performance, and it mainly determines the cooling
system and machine volume. A correct design meets both: pick the machine whose peak
curve covers the acceleration points and whose continuous curve covers the sustained points.
Passenger cars tend to be peak-driven, since their duty is mild, while buses and trucks tend to
be continuous-driven, because they spend long periods at high load.

the road. Regulators therefore moved from NEDC to WLTC, which is more dynamic.

4.13.13     Adaptive and Intelligent Control
Adaptive control adjusts controller parameters in real time as the plant changes, for example
adapting torque gain to vehicle mass, estimated from m̂ = Ft /a during acceleration, or re-
estimating R0 as the battery ages. It has low to moderate computational load, but requires
sufficient excitation to work reliably. Intelligent control covers neural networks, reinforcement
learning and related data-driven methods. A reinforcement learning agent learns a policy u =
π(x) that maximises reward, for example low energy with good tracking. Such methods can
handle complex behaviour, but require extensive data and raise verification challenges, and so
most remain in research or in supervisory, non-safety roles.

4.15      Driving Range and Energy Consumption Prediction
4.15.1    Introduction to EV Range Prediction
Range is the distance a vehicle can travel on one charge. It equals usable energy divided by
energy consumption per unit distance. The numerator is mostly a property of the battery, and
the denominator a property of the vehicle, driver, route and weather. Range prediction therefore
needs to model both, as shown in Figure 4.124. Route, speed, grade and ambient conditions go
into a vehicle model to give consumption e. SoC and health determine usable energy Eu . Their
ratio is shown to the driver, and the measured consumption continually updates the estimate.

4.15.2    Rated and Real-World Driving Range
Rated range comes from a standard test, such as MIDC in India, WLTP in Europe or the
EPA multi-cycle procedure in the USA. Results vary among cycles, because the speed profiles
differ. Real-world range depends on actual speed, traffic, terrain, climate and driver. The two
can differ widely. A rated MIDC range is typically optimistic relative to real driving, since
MIDC is gentle and is run in mild temperature with HVAC off.

the top and bottom, within an operating window:

4.15.4     Sign Conventions, Units and Assumptions
In this section:

   • Pb > 0 means power leaves the battery; Pb < 0 means power enters it.

   • Energy consumption is defined at the battery terminals, in Wh/km or kWh/100 km, with
     1 kWh/100 km = 10 Wh/km.

   • Time is in s, distance in m or km, and power in W unless otherwise shown. A division
     by 3600 converts J to Wh.

   • The window is assumed fixed, and in the case studies efficiencies are assumed constant.

Consumption stated “from the wall” includes charging losses, and is therefore higher than
terminal consumption.

4.15.7    Energy Consumption Prediction Models and Approaches
   • Physics-based prediction. Uses the equations of this unit. It needs no training data and
     extrapolates well, but requires parameters and route information [31].

   • Drive-cycle-based prediction. Selects or builds a representative cycle for the expected
     trip, then simulates it.

   • Historical energy consumption methods. These use the average over recent kilometres,
     emeas = ∆E/∆D. This is the simple “guess-o-meter” in many cars, which reacts slowly to
     route changes.

   • Statistical prediction. Fits distributions of consumption to trip features, giving a range
     with confidence bounds.


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                      127
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                     B24EES502


   • Regression-based prediction. Fits a function such as e = β0 + β1 v2 + β2 /v + β3 s + β4 Paux
     by least squares. The terms are chosen from physics, which improves extrapolation.

   • Machine learning-based prediction. Random forests and gradient boosting learn non-
     linear mappings from trip features to e [29].

   • Neural network-based range prediction. Networks, including recurrent ones, process
     speed and battery time series, and need a lot of data.

   • Hybrid physics and data-driven prediction. A physics model gives the baseline ephys
     and a learned term corrects the residual, e = ephys +gθ (x). This retains physical plausibility
     while capturing unmodelled effects.

Table 4.31 compares the two families. Physics-based models are explainable and need param-
eters. Data-driven models adapt to the fleet and need data. Hybrid methods try to take the best
of each.

Example 21: Case Study 1: Driving range from usable energy and consumption
Problem. Find the range on WLTC of the reference BEV, new and at 80% SoH, and the
