Unit 1: Introduction to Electric Vehicles
1.1     Introduction to Electric Vehicles
Road transport is being re-engineered around the electric machine. A vehicle that once relied
on a single heat engine and a mechanical gearbox is increasingly a coordinated system of an
electrochemical store, power-electronic converters, electric machines, embedded controllers
and thermal circuits. This section sets out what an electric vehicle is, how the technology
evolved, why it matters, how electrified vehicles are classified, and which components make
up the system. The later sections then quantify each of these ideas.

1.1.1    Definition and Concept of Electric Vehicles
An electric vehicle (EV) is a road vehicle in which at least part of the propulsive force at
the wheels is produced by one or more electric machines drawing energy from an on-board
electrical source. The definition is deliberately broad because the source differs among vehicle
types: a rechargeable battery, a fuel-cell stack, or a generator driven by an engine. When all the
propulsion energy comes from a battery charged from the grid, the vehicle is a pure or battery
electric vehicle.
Three engineering ideas distinguish an EV from a conventional vehicle.

   1. The energy path is mainly electrical. Energy reaches the wheels through cables and
      converters rather than through a fuel line, combustion chamber and multi-speed gearbox.
      This allows the designer to place components with much greater freedom.

   2. The energy path is bidirectional. An electric machine can act as a motor or as a gen-
      erator, so kinetic energy can return to the battery during braking. A heat engine has no
      equivalent ability.

   3. The torque source is fast and controllable. A power-electronic drive can change motor
      torque in milliseconds and can deliver full torque from standstill. Hence many EVs need
      only a single fixed reduction gear.

1.1.2    Historical Development of Electric Vehicles
The EV is older than the petrol car. Its history has three broad phases, an early rise, a long
eclipse and a modern return, and each phase was driven by the energy-storage technology
available at the time [2, 8].


Early development (1830s–1900). Small electric carriages were demonstrated in the 1830s
using non-rechargeable cells. The practical turning point was the lead-acid rechargeable cell of

School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                               10
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                    B24EES502


Gaston Planté in 1859, later improved by Camille Fauré’s pasted plates. By the 1890s electric
cabs operated in cities such as London and New York. In 1899 the electric car La Jamais
Contente of Camille Jenatzy became the first road vehicle to exceed 100 km/h [2].


Decline (1910s–1960s). EVs were quiet, clean and easy to start, but their range was limited
by the low specific energy of lead-acid batteries. Three developments favoured petrol vehicles:
cheap oil, mass production of the Ford Model T from 1908, and the electric self-starter intro-
duced in 1912, which removed the hand crank. Electric traction then survived mainly in niche
roles such as delivery floats, industrial trucks and rail.


Renewed interest (1970s–1990s). The oil shocks of the 1970s and later urban air-quality
rules revived research. California’s zero-emission vehicle mandate of 1990 prompted vehicles
such as the General Motors EV1 (1996), and the Toyota Prius (1997) brought power-split hy-
brids to mass production [8]. Power MOSFETs, IGBTs and microcontrollers made efficient
AC drives possible over the same period.


Lithium-ion era (2008 onwards). Lithium-ion cells, first commercialised for portable elec-
tronics in 1991, changed the range equation. The Tesla Roadster (2008) and the Nissan Leaf
(2010) showed that lithium-ion BEVs were viable, and costs have since fallen with scale. Ac-
cording to the International Energy Agency, global electric-car sales exceeded 20 million in
2025, about one in four new cars sold [27]. In India the Ministry of Heavy Industries’ PM
E-DRIVE scheme, with an outlay of Rs 10,900 crore, succeeded FAME-II in 2024 to support
electric two- and three-wheelers, buses, trucks and charging infrastructure [28].

1.1.3    Need and Importance of Electric Mobility
The case for electric mobility rests on several linked technical and social drivers.

   • Energy efficiency. A BEV typically converts about 70–80% of the electrical energy
     taken from the battery into energy at the wheels, whereas a petrol engine’s tank-to-wheel
     efficiency in urban use is commonly about 20–25%. Section 1.4 examines these figures.

   • Local air quality. A BEV has no tailpipe emissions, which matters most in dense cities.
     Non-exhaust particulates from brakes and tyres remain, although regenerative braking
     reduces brake wear.

   • Climate policy. Life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions depend on the grid mix and on
     battery manufacture, so the benefit grows as electricity generation decarbonises [29].

   • Energy security. Electricity can be generated from domestic and renewable sources,
     which reduces dependence on imported crude oil. This is significant for India, which
     imports most of its crude.

       • Operating cost and new functionality. Electricity per kilometre is usually cheaper

1.1.4.1   Battery Electric Vehicles (BEV)
A BEV stores all of its energy electrochemically and is propelled only by electric motors. It
has no engine, fuel tank or exhaust. The battery is recharged from the grid through an on-board
charger for AC supply or directly from an off-board DC fast charger, and partly by regenerative
braking. Its strengths are the highest well-to-wheel efficiency of the four families when the
electricity is low-carbon, mechanical simplicity and quiet operation. Its limitations are the
mass and cost of the battery and the charging time, because range scales almost linearly with
installed energy. Sections 1.2 and 1.7 examine its architecture and sizing.

1.1.4.2   Hybrid Electric Vehicles (HEV)
An HEV combines an internal combustion engine (ICE) and at least one electric machine, and
has no external charging. All net energy comes from fuel; the small battery only shifts energy
in time. The hybrid gains efficiency in four ways: it stops the engine at idle, recovers braking
energy, downsizes the engine, and runs it nearer its efficient operating region.
Hybrids are also graded by the electric machine’s contribution. In a micro hybrid an uprated
starter-generator provides stop-start. In a mild hybrid, often 48 V, the machine assists the
engine but cannot normally propel the car alone. A full hybrid can drive short distances on
electric power alone.

1.1.4.3   Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicles (PHEV)
A PHEV is a hybrid whose larger battery can be charged from the grid. It normally starts in
charge-depleting mode, using grid energy, and changes to charge-sustaining mode, behaving
like an HEV, when the battery reaches its lower SoC limit. A PHEV can cover most daily
trips electrically and still have the range of a fuel vehicle, at the price of carrying two complete
energy systems. Its real-world benefit depends strongly on how regularly it is charged. A
range-extended EV is effectively a series PHEV whose engine only drives a generator.

1.1.4.4   Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles (FCEV)
An FCEV generates electricity on board in a fuel cell, normally a proton-exchange-membrane
stack, by combining stored hydrogen with oxygen from air; the only exhaust product is water
vapour. Traction is entirely electric, so its drivetrain resembles a BEV with the battery replaced
by a stack and hydrogen tank plus a small buffer battery. Its advantages are fast refuelling and
a high energy-to-mass ratio, which suits long-haul duty. Its limitations are sparse refuelling
infrastructure, stack cost and durability, and the energy lost in producing, compressing and
reconverting hydrogen.

The advantages and limitations below apply principally to BEVs and should be read as engi-
neering trade-offs, not as absolutes.

1.2     Configuration of Electric Vehicles
The configuration of a vehicle describes how its energy sources, converters, machines and
wheels are connected. The choice fixes which energy paths exist, and therefore which operat-
ing modes are possible, how large each component must be, and how the control problem is
structured. Throughout this section electrical, mechanical and chemical paths use the colour
convention given under the diagrams.

1.2.1    Basic Architecture of an Electric Vehicle
Any EV can be regarded as three subsystems [1]. The electric propulsion subsystem comprises
the vehicle controller, power converter, motor, mechanical transmission and driven wheels.
The energy source subsystem comprises the source, its management system and the charging
unit. The auxiliary subsystem comprises power steering, climate control and the low-voltage
supply. Inputs from the accelerator and brake pedals are interpreted by the controller as torque
demands, and the converter regulates the power flowing between source and motor.

1.2.6     Fuel Cell Electric Vehicle Configuration
Figure 1.6 shows an FCEV. Hydrogen from a high-pressure tank and oxygen from the com-

cell replaces the engine-generator set.

1.2.8    Single-Motor and Multi-Motor EV Architectures
A single-motor architecture uses one machine with a differential (Fig. 1.7a,b). It is the lowest-
cost option and the efficiency map of a single machine is simple to manage. A multi-motor
architecture uses two or more machines. Two axle motors (Fig. 1.7c) give AWD, redundancy
and the chance to split torque so that both machines run efficiently. A motor at each driven
wheel, or in-wheel motors (Fig. 1.7d), replaces the mechanical differential with an electronic
one and allows torque vectoring, in which unequal left and right torque helps steer the car.
Multi-motor systems raise component count and cost and need robust fault management, be-
cause an unbalanced torque is safety-critical. In-wheel machines also increase unsprung mass,
which degrades ride quality, and they must survive the harsh wheel environment.

1.2.9    Powertrain Components and Their Functions
Table 1.3 lists the functions of each component; here their interaction is considered. In motor-
ing, the inverter imposes a voltage on the motor that produces the demanded torque; the gear
reduces speed and multiplies torque; and the differential lets the wheels turn at different speeds
in corners. Motor and inverter ratings are coupled by voltage and current: the back-EMF of the
motor must stay below what the inverter can produce from the DC bus, and the phase current
sets torque. The BMS and thermal system bound everything by supplying the allowable power
of the battery and the temperature limits of the drive.

1.3      Performance of Electric Vehicles
The performance of a vehicle is what the driver experiences as acceleration, top speed, hill-
climbing ability and range. All of these follow from one equation, Newton’s second law applied
along the road. This section derives that equation term by term, then uses it to find the torque,
power and energy that the powertrain must deliver.

1.3.1     Vehicle Performance Characteristics
Vehicle performance is normally specified by the following measures.

   • Acceleration time, usually from 0 to 100 km/h.

   • Maximum speed, on a level road and sustained.

   • Gradeability, the steepest slope the vehicle can climb at a given speed.

   • Driving range, the distance on one charge under a stated cycle.

   • Energy consumption, in Wh/km, which is the EV counterpart of fuel consumption.

The first three are power and torque requirements; the last two are energy requirements. This
separation is important in design: motor and inverter follow from the former, and the battery
energy from the latter.

1.3.3     Rolling Resistance
Rolling resistance arises mainly from hysteresis in the tyre. The tyre deforms as it enters the
contact patch and recovers as it leaves, but recovers less energy than was put in. Consequently
the normal pressure is greater in the leading half of the patch, so the resultant ground reaction
lies ahead of the axle and creates a moment opposing rotation. This moment is equivalent to a
retarding force proportional to the normal load:

                                          Fr = Crr m g cos θ,                                (1.8)

where Crr (dimensionless) is the rolling resistance coefficient, g = 9.81 m/s2 is gravitational
acceleration, and θ (rad) is the road angle. For a passenger car on asphalt Crr is about 0.008–

depends mainly on mass. It dominates at low speed.

1.3.4    Aerodynamic Drag Resistance
A moving vehicle transfers momentum to the air. Consider air of density ρ (kg/m3 ) approaching
at relative speed v. The dynamic pressure is 12 ρv2 , so a body of frontal area A (m2 ) experiences
a force proportional to 21 ρv2 A. A dimensionless drag coefficient Cd , which includes pressure
drag due to flow separation and skin friction, makes this exact:

                                     Fa = 12 ρ Cd A (v + vw )2 ,                             (1.9)

where vw (m/s) is the headwind speed (zero in still air). At sea level ρ ≈ 1.225 kg/m3 . Typical
modern EV values are Cd ≈ 0.22–0.32 and A ≈ 2.0–2.6 m2 .
The quadratic dependence is decisive. Aerodynamic power grows with the cube of speed,
Pa = Fa v, so doubling speed needs eight times the aerodynamic power. For this reason highway
range is much shorter than urban range in EVs, the reverse of the usual ICE pattern, and why
EV designers pursue low Cd A.

1.3.5    Gradient Resistance
On a slope the weight mg resolves into a component normal to the road and a component along
it. The component along the road opposes uphill motion:

                                          Fg = m g sin θ.                                   (1.10)

For small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ, so Fg ≈ mgGr /100. Thus a 1600 kg vehicle on a 10% slope
has Fg ≈ 1560 N. On a downhill section Fg is negative and helps the motion, and that energy
can be recovered. The force is conservative: the potential energy gained on the climb can in
principle be recovered on the descent.

1.4.1    Energy Flow in Battery Electric Vehicles
Energy in a BEV flows along a single chain, but in both directions. In motoring, energy flows

The mechanical chain contains bearing and gear-mesh losses, normally about 3–5% in a single-

power Pw = Ft v does not itself represent loss. It supplies the road load, which then dissipates
it as rolling hysteresis and turbulent air, or stores it as kinetic or potential energy. Some of the
stored portion is what regenerative braking can reclaim.

1.4.5     Energy Losses in Different EV Components
Each component loses energy through distinct physical mechanisms, summarised below.

    • Battery. Ohmic and polarisation losses, modelled as Ploss ≈ I 2 Rint , where Rint (Ω) is the
      pack internal resistance. Losses rise with the square of current and at low temperature.

    • Inverter. Conduction loss, proportional to current through the device on-state drop, and
      switching loss, proportional to switching frequency. Section 1.8.17 gives expressions.

    • Motor. Copper loss 3I 2 R s in the stator, iron loss from hysteresis and eddy currents (rising
      with frequency), and mechanical friction and windage.

    • Transmission. Gear-mesh friction, bearing friction and oil churning.

    • Auxiliaries. Heating and air-conditioning can draw several kilowatts, which is often
      comparable to the traction demand in slow traffic.

1.5.2    Requirements of EV Traction Batteries
Compared with consumer cells, a traction pack must meet a demanding and conflicting set of
requirements.

   • High specific energy and energy density, for range within limited mass and volume.

   • High specific power, for acceleration, regeneration and fast charging.

   • Long cycle and calendar life, typically eight years or more under warranty.

   • Safety under abuse, including crash, overcharge, short circuit and thermal runaway; stan-
     dards such as UN R100 and India’s AIS-156 apply [22, 23].

   • Operation from below freezing to high summer temperatures.

   • Low cost per kilowatt-hour, recyclability and a secure supply of materials.

State of health measures ageing relative to a new cell. It may be defined on capacity or on
resistance:

where REoL (Ω) is the resistance defining end of life. A traction battery is conventionally at end

1.5.10     Depth of Discharge (DoD)
Depth of discharge is the fraction removed from the fully charged state, DoD = 1 − SoC for
a single discharge from full. Designers also use a usable window, for example 5–95% SoC,
so that the BMS never exposes the extremes, which cause the most stress. Shallower average
cycling greatly improves life, and so a pack sized with a margin may outlast the vehicle.

1.5.11     Cycle Life and Calendar Life
Cycle life is the number of charge–discharge cycles before end of life under specified condi-
tions; it depends on DoD, C-rate and temperature. Calendar life is the ageing that occurs with
time alone, mainly by growth of the solid-electrolyte interphase on the anode. Calendar ageing
accelerates with temperature and with high state of charge, so storing a vehicle at high SoC in
heat is harmful. A useful sizing estimate links cycle life and lifetime distance:

                                          Dlife ≈ Ncyc R ,                                  (1.41)

where Ncyc is the number of full-equivalent cycles and R (km) the usable range per cycle. For
example, 1500 cycles at 300 km corresponds to 450 000 km.

1.5.13                          Battery Pack Configuration
Pack design selects cell format and arrangement. Three formats are used: cylindrical cells, such

Whatever the format, the pack must also provide contactors, fuses, a pre-charge circuit, current
and voltage sensing, and structural crash protection, as listed in Fig. 1.14.

1.5.15                                 Lithium-Ion Battery Chemistry
A lithium-ion cell (Fig. 1.14, left) stores energy by moving lithium ions between two host
materials, a mechanism called intercalation. The anode is commonly graphite, sometimes
with silicon added, on a copper foil. The cathode is a lithium metal oxide or phosphate on
an aluminium foil. A porous separator keeps the electrodes apart while the organic liquid
electrolyte conducts ions. During discharge, lithium ions leave the anode and intercalate into
the cathode, while electrons travel through the external circuit and deliver power. Charging
reverses the process.
For a layered-oxide cathode, the reactions on discharge are

                                                                                                          → LiMO2 ,                                  (1.45)

where M denotes transition metals such as Ni, Mn, Co or Al. The cell voltage is the difference
between the electrochemical potentials of the two electrodes, so the cathode chemistry defines
most of the voltage and capacity [14].

1.5.17     Battery Management System (BMS)
The BMS is the embedded system that keeps every cell inside its safe operating area and tells
the vehicle what the battery can do. Figure 1.17 shows a distributed architecture. Slave boards
attached to each module measure cell voltages and temperatures and perform cell balancing.
They communicate over an isolated link with a master controller. The master also reads pack
current, runs estimation and fault logic, and controls the contactors and pre-charge. It reports
allowable charge and discharge power to the VCU and charger over CAN, and requests cooling
or heating from the thermal loop.

   3. Protection: over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current, over-temperature and isolation
      faults, by derating or by opening contactors.

1.5.18      Battery Thermal Management
Lithium-ion cells perform best within a narrow temperature window, commonly about 15–
35 ◦ C. Too cold, and resistance rises and fast charging risks lithium plating. Too hot, and ageing
accelerates and, past the onset temperature, thermal runaway may start. The heat generated in
a pack is approximately

1.5.19      Battery Selection Considerations for EV Applications
Battery selection follows the duty. The principal considerations are as follows.

   • Energy requirement: range target and vehicle consumption (Eq. (1.22)).


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                                      42
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                   B24EES502


   • Power requirement: the peak discharge and charge power expressed as C-rate.

   • Voltage: set by the motor and inverter design and by fast-charging needs.

   • Life: daily distance, climate, and warranty target.

   • Safety and regulation: chemistry stability and homologation requirements.

   • Packaging and mass: available floor area and height.

   • Cost and supply: cell price and material availability.

A simple rule is that the power-to-energy ratio (in h−1 , numerically a C-rate) identifies the class
of cell needed. A long-range car with 100 kW and 60 kWh needs only 1.7C, so a high-energy
cell suffices. A small hybrid with 30 kW and 1.5 kWh needs 20C, so it needs a high-power cell.
In Indian conditions, high ambient temperature and cost sensitivity favour LFP for electric
three-wheelers, buses and mass-market cars, whereas NMC remains common where range per
kilogram matters most.


1.6     Drive Cycle and Its Importance in System Analysis
1.6.1    Definition of a Drive Cycle
A drive cycle is a standardised or measured record of vehicle speed against time, v(t), that
represents a type of driving. It defines the accelerations, cruises, decelerations and stops that
a vehicle must follow. A regulatory cycle is performed on a chassis dynamometer under con-
trolled conditions so that vehicles can be compared on an equal basis. Sometimes the term also
includes a road gradient profile, θ(t), but the standard chassis cycles assume a level road.

1.6.2    Importance of Drive Cycles in EV Design
Section 1.3 gave instantaneous equations; a drive cycle provides the time history that turns
them into design figures. It is used in the following ways.

   • Regulation and labelling: energy consumption and range are homologated on a stated
     cycle.

   • Component sizing: peak and root-mean-square torque and power fix motor and inverter
     ratings, and cycle energy fixes the battery.

   • Efficiency evaluation: operating-point distribution over a motor map predicts average
     efficiency.

   • Thermal and durability design: current profiles drive thermal models and battery age-
     ing tests.



School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                 43
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                                                  B24EES502


             • Control development: energy management and regeneration strategies are compared
               over identical conditions, for example in NREL’s ADVISOR or MathWorks Powertrain
               Blockset [17, 30].

No regulatory cycle exactly represents real driving; the choice of cycle changes the declared
range, so the same car has different ranges under MIDC, WLTC and EPA tests. Quoted range

1.6.4.1             Indian Driving Cycle (IDC)
The IDC is a short modal cycle for two- and three-wheelers in India, based on observed low-
speed traffic. It comprises six repeats of a 108 s cycle, giving 648 s and about 3.9 km at an
average of about 21.9 km/h, with a peak speed of about 42 km/h [20]. It is widely quoted for
electric scooters, so its low speed gives ranges well above what a rider would obtain on faster
roads.

1.6.4.2             Modified Indian Driving Cycle (MIDC)
The MIDC is the Indian cycle for passenger cars, with a structure like NEDC: four repetitions
of a 195 s urban section followed by a 400 s extra-urban section. The total duration is 1180 s
and the distance about 10.7 km. The extra-urban maximum speed is 90 km/h [21], a limit that
differs from NEDC. Because it is gentle, MIDC ranges are generally optimistic compared with
WLTC ranges for the same car.

1.7.4    Selection of Rolling Resistance Coefficient
Crr is chosen from tyre data, typically 0.008–0.012 for low-rolling-resistance passenger tyres
on good roads. A conservative value should be used for poor road surfaces or for commercial
tyres. Tyre grip, comfort and noise compete with low Crr , so it is not freely reducible.

1.7.5    Selection of Aerodynamic Drag Coefficient
Cd depends on body shape and is established by CFD and wind-tunnel testing. Benchmarks
are about 0.22–0.30 for sedans and 0.30–0.38 for SUVs. Closed front grilles, flat underbodies,
aero wheels and active shutters are the standard measures in BEVs, because the BEV needs far
less cooling air than an ICE car.

1.7.15     Battery Voltage Selection
Bus voltage is a system-level compromise. Higher voltage reduces current, cable mass and

and higher-voltage devices. Passenger cars generally use nominal voltages of about 350–400 V,
or about 800 V in fast-charging platforms; two-wheelers commonly use 48–96 V. The designer
must check that the motor’s back-EMF at top speed can be handled at the minimum pack
voltage.

1.7.18     DC-DC Converter Selection
The DC–DC converter is sized for the total low-voltage load, which includes ECUs, lights,

Paux /VLV . It must operate across the full input range between Vmin and Vmax of the pack, and
provide galvanic isolation between HV and chassis-referenced LV networks.

1.7.19     Regenerative Braking Considerations
Regenerative braking influences sizing in three respects. The motor and inverter are normally
symmetric in torque, but the battery’s charge limit is usually smaller than its discharge limit, so
charge acceptance often sets the maximum regen power. Thus at high SoC or low temperature
regeneration may be cut sharply. The friction brake system must still handle a full stop without
regeneration. And brake blending must respect tyre adhesion distribution and stability; the
front axle takes more brake force, which is a reason that RWD vehicles cannot regenerate all
braking.

1.7.20     Safety Margins and Design Constraints
Beyond performance, the design must satisfy the following constraints.

   • Thermal limits of motor windings, magnets, semiconductor junctions and cells.

   • Tyre adhesion limit, Eq. (1.4).

   • Motor voltage limit at top speed and minimum SoC.

   • Package and mass budget, including axle load limits.

   • HV safety: insulation, interlocks, contactors, and crash disconnection.

   • Margins for ageing: capacity fade, resistance rise, and bus-capacitor degradation.

1.8     Three-Phase Inverter
1.8.1    Introduction to Power Inverters
An inverter is a power-electronic converter that converts DC power into AC power with con-
trollable voltage, frequency and phase. It works by switching semiconductor devices on and off
rapidly, so that the output is a sequence of voltage pulses whose average over a short interval
follows the desired waveform. Devices operate as near-ideal switches, either fully on or fully


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                 58
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                  B24EES502


off, which keeps the loss small even at high power: a switch ideally has either zero voltage or
zero current, and so dissipates no power [4].

1.8.2    Role of Inverters in EV Powertrains
The traction inverter sits between the DC battery and the AC motor (Fig. 1.2). It performs four
functions.

   1. It produces the variable-frequency, variable-voltage supply that sets motor speed and
      torque.

   2. It carries out the fast current control that makes torque response nearly instantaneous.

   3. It reverses power flow for regenerative braking, with no extra hardware, because each
      switch has an antiparallel diode.

   4. It protects the machine and battery against over-current, over-voltage and over-
      temperature.

Its efficiency directly enters Eq. (1.26), and it must withstand high current, automotive vibration
and coolant temperatures. Silicon IGBTs have long been the norm at 400 V, and SiC MOS-
FETs are spreading quickly because their lower switching loss suits higher voltage and higher
frequency.

1.8.3    Classification of Inverters
Inverters are classified according to several attributes.

   • By number of output phases: single-phase, three-phase, or multiphase.

   • By source type: voltage source (VSI) or current source (CSI).

   • By number of output levels: two-level, or multilevel such as neutral-point-clamped.

   • By control method: six-step (square wave) or pulse-width modulated.

   • By commutation: hard-switched or soft-switched (resonant).

1.8.4    Single-Phase and Three-Phase Inverters
A single-phase full-bridge has two legs, and its output alternates across one load. Instantaneous
single-phase power pulsates at twice the output frequency. A balanced three-phase system
instead delivers constant instantaneous power,

                            p(t) = va ia + vb ib + vc ic = 3Vrms Irms cos φ,                (1.65)

where v x and i x are the instantaneous phase voltages and currents. Equation (1.65) is the reason
three-phase machines produce smooth torque and are universal in EV traction, with single-
phase inverters confined to low-power uses such as portable loads or vehicle-to-load outlets.

1.8.5     Voltage Source Inverter (VSI)
A VSI is fed from a stiff DC voltage, usually a battery in parallel with a low-impedance ca-
pacitor. Its output voltage waveform is set by the switching pattern, while its output current
depends on the load. All traction inverters are of this type, because a battery is naturally a
voltage source. The DC-link capacitor supplies the high-frequency current pulses drawn by the
switching bridge, so that the battery and its cables see a smooth current. A leg must never have
both switches on: that would short-circuit the DC link, an event called shoot-through.

1.8.6     Current Source Inverter (CSI)
A CSI is fed from a stiff DC current, obtained by placing a large inductor in series with the
source. Its switches steer this current through the phases, so that its output current waveform
is defined and the voltage depends on the load. Its switches must block reverse voltage, and
it has inherent short-circuit robustness. However, the large DC inductor is heavy and lossy,
and the regulation of current needs an additional front-end stage. Table 1.13 summarises the
comparison, and it shows why the VSI dominates in EVs.

1.8.7     Two-Level Three-Phase VSI
A two-level VSI connects each motor terminal to one of two voltages at any instant, positive
or negative DC rail. It is simple, compact and well understood, and its six switches make it the
most widely used traction topology. Multilevel inverters reduce device stress and ripple but are
justified mainly at higher voltages, so they are seldom used in 400 V cars, though they are of
growing interest at 800 V.

1.8.15        120-Degree and 180-Degree Conduction Modes
180-degree conduction. Each switch conducts for 180◦ of the period, and three switches
are on at any time, one per leg. This is the mode of Fig. 1.23: complete complementary leg
operation with all terminals tied to a rail. Output voltage does not depend on load, as long
as both devices of each leg are driven. A switch turning off and its leg partner turning on are
simultaneous, so a dead time is required.


120-degree conduction. Each switch conducts for 120◦ , and only two switches are on at any
instant, one upper and one lower in different legs. The third phase floats for 60◦ intervals,
which gives a natural commutation gap between switches in one leg and hence a safety margin.
The phase voltage is a quasi-square wave of amplitude Vdc /2 for a resistive star load, and the
floating terminal voltage depends on the load. This mode is the basis of trapezoidal brushless
DC drives, where the third phase’s back-EMF is used to sense the rotor position. For the same
Vdc , however, the mode gives a smaller fundamental, as derived next.

1.9.2    Importance of PWM in EV Motor Drives
PWM is what allows the fixed battery voltage to become a smooth, adjustable AC supply. It
matters in EV drives for these reasons.

   • Amplitude and frequency of the output are controlled independently, which allows fast
     torque and flux control.

   • Low-order harmonics are eliminated and distortion is moved near the switching fre-
     quency, reducing torque ripple and loss.

   • Fast response is obtained, since the voltage is updated every carrier period.

   • It is available from any automotive microcontroller via dedicated timer peripherals.

1.9.3    Switching Frequency
The switching frequency f s = 1/T s (Hz) is the rate at which a leg’s devices switch, set in
carrier PWM by the carrier frequency, so f s = fc . It trades ripple against loss, Eq. (1.79). It
also influences audible noise: values within the human hearing band, up to 10 kHz or so, can
be objectionable, especially in quiet EVs. Control bandwidth is limited to a small fraction of
f s , usually by a factor of about 10.

1.9.9    Unipolar and Bipolar PWM Concepts
The terms bipolar and unipolar arise chiefly in the single-phase full bridge.

   • Bipolar PWM. Diagonal switch pairs are driven together, so the output swings between
     +Vdc and −Vdc . Harmonics appear around fc .

   • Unipolar PWM. Each leg has its own reference, of opposite sign, compared with the
     same carrier. Output steps between 0 and ±Vdc , and the effective ripple frequency at the
     output is doubled to 2 fc , while the same switching loss is maintained.

In a three-phase VSI, each leg is inherently two-level, or bipolar with respect to O. But the line
voltage, as the difference of two poles, has unipolar character: it takes the values 0, ±Vdc within
any half cycle, which is visible in the vab trace of Fig. 1.25.

1.9.14    Advantages and Limitations of SPWM
Advantages.

   • Simple to implement, with one comparison per leg and low processor load.

   • Linear voltage gain up to ma = 1.

   • Fixed switching frequency and a predictable harmonic spectrum.


Limitations.

   • Only 78.5% of the six-step fundamental is available in the linear range.

   • Each phase is treated independently, so the three-phase structure of the load is not ex-
     ploited.

   • More switching and more current ripple than optimised sequences at the same frequency
     for high modulation indices.

   • Non-linear overmodulation behaviour.

The bus limitation can be overcome by adding a common-mode (zero-sequence) offset to all
references. Because the motor neutral is isolated, such an offset leaves line voltages unchanged.
Third-harmonic injection and the min-max offset are examples [10], and the latter is the carrier-
based equivalent of SVPWM.


1.10      Space Vector Pulse Width Modulation (SVPWM)
1.10.1    Introduction to Space Vector Representation
Rather than modulating each phase independently, SVPWM treats the inverter and motor as a
single three-phase system [9]. It represents the three phase voltages by one rotating vector and
synthesises it from the eight states of Table 1.14. This viewpoint is natural for AC machines,
because the three stator currents produce a single rotating magnetic field, whose direction and
magnitude are what really matter. It also fits field-oriented control, which outputs a voltage
vector, and gains a higher DC-bus utilisation than SPWM.

1.10.4     Alpha-Beta Reference Frame
The αβ frame is a stationary, orthogonal, two-axis frame in the plane of the stator, with α

the Clarke and Park transformations to a rotor-fixed dq frame, in which torque and flux are
nearly DC quantities and are regulated by PI controllers. The resulting voltage command re-
turns to αβ, which is the input to SVPWM.

1.10.20     Applications in EV Traction Drives
SVPWM or its carrier-based equivalent is the dominant modulator in PMSM traction drives

Reference [5] gives an extensive treatment of these drive applications.


1.11      Hysteresis Current Control
1.11.1     Introduction to Hysteresis Current Control
PWM methods generate a voltage and rely on an outer loop to regulate current. Motor torque
depends on current, so an alternative is to control the current directly. Hysteresis current control
(HCC), or bang-bang control, does this with no modulator at all: it compares the measured
phase current with its reference and switches the leg whenever the error leaves a tolerance
band [11]. It has the fastest possible response for a given inverter, since it acts on each error as
it occurs. Its drawback, examined below, is a variable switching frequency.

1.11.2     Operating Principle
Consider phase a of Fig. 1.21. With S 1 on, a positive voltage across the winding drives current
up; with S 4 on, a negative voltage drives it down. The controller holds the current within a
band around the reference by choosing between these two states. It behaves in the same way as
a thermostat, which allows the room temperature to wander within limits. Figure 1.31 shows
the mechanism over a few switching cycles.

1.11.4     Hysteresis Band
The hysteresis band is the permitted error range ±h, with total width ∆I = 2h (A). As h de-
creases, tracking improves, but the switching frequency increases, so h is chosen by trading off
current ripple against inverter switching loss. It must also be large compared to current sensor
noise, or spurious switching will result.

1.11.10       Advantages and Limitations
Advantages.

    • Very fast dynamic response, limited only by inductance and available voltage.

    • Simple, robust structure that does not require a motor model.

    • Inherent peak-current limitation, which protects devices.

    • Automatic compensation for DC-bus variation, back-EMF and dead time.




School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                87
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                  B24EES502


Limitations.

   • Variable switching frequency, with a spread harmonic spectrum and acoustic noise.

   • Switching loss cannot be fixed, making thermal design more conservative.

   • Interaction among phases for an isolated neutral.

   • Digital implementation needs very high sampling rates: a loop at fsample overshoots the
     band, by up to T sample di/dt.

   • Higher ripple at low speed for a given band, and poor low-current behaviour near zero
     crossings.

1.11.11     Applications in Electric Vehicle Motor Drives
HCC is well-suited to drives in which torque response and simplicity matter more than har-
monic quality. It has been used for switched-reluctance traction motors, in which phase current
profiling is natural, and for low-cost brushless DC drives in light electric vehicles. In main-
stream PMSM cars, fixed-frequency FOC with SVPWM is preferred, for reasons of NVH and
EMC, but HCC concepts remain as a fast peak-current limit. Direct torque control is a related
method, which applies hysteresis to torque and flux rather than to currents [5].

1.11.12     Comparison with Carrier-Based PWM and SVPWM
HCC differs from the previous two methods in one basic respect: SPWM and SVPWM are
voltage modulators with a fixed period, inside a current loop; HCC is itself the current loop,
with no fixed period. So carrier methods offer predictable spectra and losses, but response is
limited by sampling and PI bandwidth. HCC reverses that trade-off. The complete comparison
follows in Section 1.12.

1.12.1      Discussion of the Comparison
Three conclusions can be drawn from Table 1.19. First, SVPWM dominates SPWM in nearly
every respect, at the small cost of a few extra calculations; and since a carrier-based form with
a zero-sequence offset (Eq. (1.112)) performs identically, the two are best seen as members of
one family. Second, HCC is a fundamentally different choice, rather than an improved version:
its strength in current response is paid for in harmonic and thermal unpredictability. Third, DC
bus utilisation carries particular value in a BEV, because battery voltage falls with SoC and the
highest-speed capability depends on the voltage margin, as shown in Eq. (1.110).

At low speed and high torque, back-EMF is small and modulation index is small, so voltage
headroom is irrelevant, whereas current ripple and audible noise matter because little road
or wind noise masks them. Continuous SVPWM at a fixed frequency is therefore preferred.
SVPWM and SPWM behave similarly at this point.
In urban and highway cruise, the drive runs at part load for most of its life. Loss reduction
determines the range, so discontinuous SVPWM is attractive at moderate to high modulation,
where the clamped leg reduces switching loss.
At high speed in the field-weakening region, the inverter runs out of voltage. SVPWM’s extra
15% is valuable, and overmodulation towards six-step gives the highest torque per ampere at
the top of the speed range, at the expense of low-order harmonics. As m f falls, synchronisation
of the switching pattern with the fundamental is needed.
In fast transients and faults, the hysteresis principle retains its value. Even in drives using
SVPWM, a hardware comparator commonly limits peak current on a cycle-by-cycle basis,
because it responds in microseconds, without waiting for the next sampling instant.
For cost-sensitive auxiliaries such as coolant pumps and small compressors, SPWM’s simplic-
ity is an advantage, and bus utilisation is less important. For switched-reluctance or trapezoidal
BLDC drives, the required current pulses suit hysteresis control, which is preferred.
For the 100 kW traction drive of Example 6, the appropriate choice is therefore field-oriented
control with SVPWM at a fixed 10 kHz, using discontinuous modulation at higher modulation
indices, overmodulation at top speed, and a hardware hysteresis comparator for peak-current
protection.
