The traction battery is the largest, heaviest and most expensive component of a battery electric
vehicle (BEV). Its energy sets the range, its power sets the acceleration and regenerative capa-
bility, its voltage sets the architecture of the inverter and charger, and its ageing sets the service
life of the vehicle. This unit develops the subject in the order an engineer meets it: selecting a
storage technology, understanding how a cell works, sizing a pack, estimating its internal states,
modelling it, managing it through a battery management system (BMS), and finally charging
it.


Notation and sign convention. Throughout this unit the battery current I is taken as positive
during discharge and negative during charge. State of charge is written as z (or SoC) and is
dimensionless, 0 ≤ z ≤ 1. Capacity Q is quoted in ampere-hours (Ah); wherever it appears
with time in seconds it is converted to coulombs using 1 Ah = 3600 C. Specific quantities are
per kilogram and density quantities are per litre.


2.1     Ragone Plot
2.1.1    Introduction to the Ragone Plot
Every energy storage device must answer two independent questions: how much energy can it
hold, and how fast can that energy be delivered? A single number cannot answer both. The
Ragone plot, named after D. V. Ragone, who used it in 1968 to compare battery systems for
electrically powered vehicles [1], answers both at once by plotting the specific power of a
device against its specific energy on logarithmic axes. Its theoretical basis was later formalised
by Christen and Carlen [2].

2.1.2    Definition and Significance
A Ragone plot is a log–log chart with specific energy on one axis and specific power on the
other. Each storage technology occupies a region, not a point, because a real device delivers less
energy when it is discharged at higher power. The chart is significant because a single glance
shows whether a technology can meet a vehicle’s energy requirement and power requirement
with an acceptable mass, and how long a discharge can be sustained.

2.1.7    Battery and Supercapacitor Performance Characteristics
A battery stores energy in chemical bonds, so charge must cross an interface and diffuse through
solids. These are slow, thermally activated steps, which limit power. A supercapacitor stores
energy in the electric double layer, with no bulk chemical reaction, so its power is limited
chiefly by its low series resistance. Its stored energy is E = 12 CV 2 , which falls with the square
of the voltage; consequently a supercapacitor bank needs a DC-DC converter to use its energy
fully. It also has a high self-discharge rate compared with a lithium-ion battery.

2.1.10       Limitations of Ragone Plot-Based Comparisons
The Ragone plot is a screening tool. Its limitations should be stated explicitly:

    • It says nothing about cost, cycle life, calendar life, safety or charging capability.



School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                              17
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                                 B24EES502


   • Published regions mix cell, module and system-level data; comparing them directly is
     misleading.

   • The curve assumes constant-power discharge at one temperature, whereas a vehicle sees
     a dynamic profile over a wide temperature range.

   • The curves shift with state of charge and ageing, as Section 2.5 shows.

   • It ignores volume unless a separate volumetric chart is drawn.

   • It ignores charge acceptance. Regenerative braking and fast charging are limited by dif-
     ferent mechanisms from discharge.


2.2     Battery Types and Battery Pack
2.2.1    Introduction to Electrochemical Batteries
An electrochemical battery converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy through
spatially separated oxidation and reduction reactions. Strictly, a cell is the basic electrochemical
unit and a battery is a connected group of cells, although common usage calls either a battery.

2.2.3.1   Lead-Acid Batteries
A lead-acid cell has a lead dioxide positive plate, a sponge lead negative plate and a sulphuric
acid electrolyte. On discharge both plates convert to lead sulphate:

The technology is low in cost and highly recyclable, and it has high surge capability for its
cost, which suits engine starting. However, it has low specific energy, poor deep-cycle life, and
suffers sulphation if left discharged. It survives in EVs mainly as the 12 V auxiliary battery and
in low-speed vehicles.

2.2.3.2   Nickel-Cadmium Batteries
A NiCd cell uses nickel oxyhydroxide and cadmium in an alkaline potassium hydroxide elec-
trolyte, with a nominal voltage of 1.2 V. It is robust, tolerates abuse and works at low tempera-
tures, but cadmium is toxic and the cell shows the memory effect. Its use in vehicles has been
effectively abandoned, largely for environmental reasons.

2.2.3.3   Nickel-Metal Hydride Batteries
NiMH replaces cadmium with a hydrogen-absorbing metal alloy. It keeps the 1.2 V nominal
voltage but offers higher capacity and better environmental acceptability. It was used widely
in hybrid vehicles due to its long shallow-cycle life and tolerance of overcharge. Its drawbacks
are high self-discharge, a low coulombic efficiency at high SoC, and heat generation at the end
of charge.

2.2.3.4   Lithium-Ion Batteries
Lithium-ion cells shuttle lithium ions between two host materials (Section 2.4). With a nominal
voltage of about 3.2–3.7 V for graphite-based cells, they lead in specific energy and offer high
coulombic efficiency and long cycle life. They need a BMS for safe operation because they
tolerate no overcharge and can enter thermal runaway.

2.2.3.5   Lithium-Polymer Batteries
The term “lithium-polymer” is used loosely. Commercial cells so described are normally
lithium-ion cells with a gelled polymer electrolyte or simply a pouch format. The chemistry


School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                               19
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                     B24EES502


is otherwise the same; the benefit is packaging flexibility and low mass. Genuine solid poly-
mer electrolyte cells, which usually run at elevated temperature, are a different and much rarer
technology.

2.2.3.6     Sodium-Ion Batteries
Sodium-ion cells follow the same rocking-chair principle, replacing lithium with sodium.
Sodium is abundant and the cathode can avoid nickel and cobalt, and aluminium can replace
copper as the negative collector. Hard carbon replaces graphite since sodium does not interca-
late into graphite adequately. Specific energy is currently below that of LFP, although the cells
show good low-temperature performance, so they are attractive for low-cost, shorter-range
vehicles and stationary storage [5].

2.2.3.7     Solid-State Batteries
Solid-state batteries replace the flammable liquid electrolyte with a solid ionic conductor (sul-
phide, oxide or polymer), which may enable a lithium metal negative electrode and therefore
higher specific energy. Open challenges include interfacial resistance, dendrite growth, stack
pressure and manufacturing scale [6]. They should be treated as an emerging technology;
claimed values for pre-commercial cells are not comparable with mass-production data.

2.2.5     Requirements of EV Traction Batteries
A traction battery must satisfy a combination of requirements:

   • high specific energy and energy density, for range and packaging;

   • high specific power, for acceleration and regenerative braking;

   • long cycle and calendar life, ideally matching the vehicle life;

   • a wide operating temperature range;

   • intrinsic and system-level safety under abuse, crash and fault conditions;

   • fast charge acceptance without lithium plating;

   • low cost per kWh, and recyclable, responsibly sourced materials.

No chemistry maximises all of these, and the art of battery engineering lies in the compromise.

2.2.7.1   Battery Enclosure and Mechanical Protection
The enclosure protects the cells from crash loads, road debris, water and dust, and contains fire
or venting events. It is commonly an aluminium or steel tray with a sealed lid, and is frequently
rated around IP67. Modern designs make it part of the vehicle body structure.

2.2.7.2   Electrical Interconnections and Busbars
Busbars are copper or aluminium conductors joining cells and modules. Their cross-section is
designed for peak current and temperature rise, and their joints (laser welded or bolted) must
retain low resistance over the vehicle life, because a poor joint is a concentrated heat source.

2.2.7.3   Thermal Management Provisions
Cell ageing and safety depend strongly on temperature, so packs normally have cold plates,

typically 20–40◦ C, and a low temperature spread across cells, typically a few degrees.

2.3       Basic Battery Operation
2.3.1     Electrochemical Energy Conversion
A cell produces electricity because the two electrode materials have different electrochemical
potentials. When an external circuit is closed, a spontaneous reaction proceeds, but it is divided
into two half-reactions at separate electrodes. Electrons cannot travel through the electrolyte,
so they are forced around the external circuit to do work, whereas the ions complete the circuit
inside the cell.

2.3.2     Construction: Electrodes, Electrolyte, Separator and Collectors
Figure 2.11 shows the universal structure of a cell.

   • The anode is the electrode where oxidation occurs and the cathode the one where reduc-
     tion occurs. These labels strictly interchange during charge, so in rechargeable cells it is
     safest to speak of the negative and positive electrodes. By industry convention, “anode”
     means the negative electrode and “cathode” the positive, named for discharge.

   • The electrolyte is an ionic conductor and electronic insulator.

   • The separator is a porous, electronically insulating membrane that prevents electrode
     contact while letting ions pass.

   • The current collectors are metal foils that carry electrons between the electrode material
     and the terminals.

2.4.1    Introduction to Lithium-Ion Technology
Lithium has the lowest electrochemical potential of the metals and low atomic mass, which
makes it attractive for high voltage and high specific energy. Using lithium metal directly
proved unsafe due to dendrites, so the lithium-ion cell instead keeps lithium in ionic form

graphite layers keep their structure while lithium enters and leaves the gaps between them.

Ion transport comprises solid diffusion in the active particles, charge transfer at the particle

depleted near the positive electrode on discharge, and at low temperatures diffusion slows dra-

2.4.6    SEI Layer and its Importance
During the first charge, the electrolyte reduces on the graphite surface at low potential, forming
the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) [10]. A good SEI passes Li+ but blocks electrons and
solvent, stopping further electrolyte decomposition, so it is essential for stability. Its formation
consumes some cyclable lithium, which is the origin of the first-cycle capacity loss. Over
life the SEI thickens slowly, which is the main mechanism of calendar ageing and resistance
growth [11].

2.4.12    Lithium Plating
On charge, the graphite potential falls. If it reaches that of metallic lithium, Li+ deposits as
metal on the particle surface instead of intercalating [12]. This occurs at high charge rate,

through the separator, and is the main reason fast charging is restricted at low temperature and

2.4.13     Thermal Runaway Fundamentals
Thermal runaway is a self-accelerating exothermic failure [13]. Triggers include mechanical
damage, an internal short, overcharge or external heating. As temperature rises, a sequence
follows: the SEI decomposes, the anode reacts with electrolyte, the separator melts allowing an
internal short, the cathode releases oxygen, and the electrolyte burns. Each stage feeds the next
because heat generation grows exponentially with temperature (Arrhenius behaviour), whereas
heat rejection grows only about linearly. The danger at pack level is propagation between cells.
Hence the BMS must detect the early signs and the pack must be designed to delay propagation.

2.4.14     Important Lithium-Ion Chemistries
Lithium iron phosphate (LFP). LiFePO4 cathode, graphite anode, nominal 3.2 V. The
strong P–O bond resists oxygen release, giving excellent thermal stability, long cycle life and
low cost, with no cobalt or nickel. Its drawbacks are a lower specific energy and voltage, a flat
OCV, and poor low-temperature performance. It is used widely in buses, entry-level cars and,
increasingly, mainstream cars.


Lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC). LiNi x Mny Coz O2 , graphite anode, nominal
3.6–3.7 V. The ratio is denoted NMC111, NMC622, NMC811 by proportion; more nickel gives
higher capacity but less thermal stability, and less cobalt reduces cost. It is the mainstream of
long-range BEVs.


Lithium nickel cobalt aluminium oxide (NCA). LiNi0.8 Co0.15 Al0.05 O2 -type cathode, nom-
inal about 3.6 V. Offers very high specific energy, but it needs careful thermal control, and is
less tolerant of abuse.


Lithium cobalt oxide (LCO). LiCoO2 , nominal about 3.7 V. It has high volumetric energy
density but cobalt-related cost and limited thermal stability, so it is used in consumer electronics
rather than traction.


Lithium titanate (LTO). Li4 Ti5 O12 anode with NMC or LMO cathode, nominal about 2.3–
2.4 V. The anode operates at about 1.55 V versus Li, which avoids plating and SEI growth, so
it has very long life and high charge rates, even in the cold. Its low voltage gives low specific
energy and high cost per kWh, restricting it to specialised buses and high-cycle applications.

2.5.3    Self-Discharge
Self-discharge is the slow loss of stored charge at open circuit due to internal side reactions.
It is small for lithium-ion, on the order of a few per cent per month, and larger when hot. It
matters mostly for long parked periods and for the imbalance it creates between cells.

2.7.1.1   Definition and Physical Interpretation
SoC is the ratio of the charge remaining to the charge available at full, on the present capacity:

                                                     Qrem
                                                z=        .                                     (2.49)
                                                     Qn

Physically, it measures the lithium concentration in the electrodes relative to the limits of use.
It is the battery analogue of a fuel gauge, with one crucial difference: the capacity of the “tank”
shrinks with age and varies with temperature.

2.7.1.2   Importance in EV Operation and Measurement Challenges
SoC is needed for the range display, for preventing overcharge and over-discharge, for setting
regen and power limits, and for balancing. It is difficult to estimate accurately because of the
following:

   • the OCV curve is flat for some chemistries, especially LFP;

   • OCV can be measured only after long rest, and it shows hysteresis;

   • current sensors have offset, gain error and drift;

   • capacity and resistance change with temperature and ageing;

   • a pack comprises many cells that are not identical.

2.7.1.5    Model-Based and Equivalent Circuit-Based Estimation
A model-based estimator runs a battery model in parallel with the real battery. The difference
between measured and predicted voltage is then used to correct the states. This is a closed-loop
observer, and unlike Coulomb counting, it can recover from a wrong initial SoC. Using the
equivalent circuit model of Section 2.11 keeps the computation feasible for a BMS microcon-
troller.

2.7.1.6    Kalman Filter
The Kalman filter (KF) is the optimal recursive estimator of the states of a linear system with
Gaussian noise. It treats the problem as a weighted average of two uncertain sources: the model
prediction, and the measurement. It sets the weight (the gain) according to their covariances.
Since the battery output depends on the nonlinear OCV(z), the basic KF needs extending.

Assumptions, advantages and disadvantages. EKF assumes a sufficiently accurate model,
approximately Gaussian noise, and small linearisation error. It corrects initial errors and pro-
vides an error bound. On the other hand it requires tuning of Q and Rv and more computation,
and its accuracy depends on the model parameters, which vary with temperature and age.

2.7.1.8    Unscented Kalman Filter
Rather than linearising, the UKF propagates a small set of deterministically chosen sigma
points through the true nonlinear functions, and recovers the mean and covariance from them.
It needs no Jacobians and copes better with strong nonlinearity, at the cost of more function
evaluations per step.

2.7.1.9    Adaptive Estimation Techniques
Adaptive filters update the noise covariances or the model parameters online. Examples include
adaptive EKF, dual EKF (one filter for states, another for parameters), and fusion with recursive
least squares. They mitigate the parameter drift with ageing, but they increase the risk of
instability, and need careful design.

2.7.1.10    Machine Learning-Based SoC Estimation
Data-driven methods learn the map from measured voltage, current and temperature to SoC,
using neural networks such as feed-forward or long short-term memory networks, or support
vector regression. They need no explicit electrochemical model, but require large, representa-
tive training data, generalise poorly outside it, and are harder to certify for safety. Hybrids that
combine a learned model with a physics-based filter are an active research area [19].

2.7.1.11    Effect of Temperature and Ageing; Initial SoC and Sensor Errors
Temperature changes OCV slightly, and changes capacity and resistance significantly, so model
parameters must be scheduled on temperature. Ageing reduces Qn ; if the SoC calculation keeps
using the new-cell value, the gauge will read high. Initial SoC uncertainty arises from long
rest with self-discharge and from the hysteresis of OCV, and it is corrected by a closed-loop
estimator. Sensor errors have three forms: current offset, which causes drift by (2.52); gain

     also gives a confidence interval.

   • Artificial neural networks (ANN) capture nonlinear relations, whereas recurrent forms
     learn time dependence.

   • Support vector machines (SVM) in regression form work with comparatively little data.

2.11      Modelling of Battery: RC Equivalent Network Model
2.11.1    Introduction and Purpose of Battery Models
A battery model predicts terminal voltage, and internal states, from the applied current. In EV
engineering, models serve four purposes: vehicle-level simulation of range and performance;
design of the BMS estimators of Section 2.7; pack design, including thermal and balancing
studies; and virtual testing of control software in MATLAB/Simulink before hardware exists
[24].

2.11.2    Electrochemical Models versus Equivalent Circuit Models
Electrochemical models, such as the Doyle–Fuller–Newman model, solve coupled partial dif-
ferential equations for concentration and potential. They reveal internal states such as the an-
ode potential, which is valuable for plating-free fast charging, but they require many hard-to-
measure parameters and heavy computation. Equivalent circuit models (ECMs) instead repro-
duce the terminal behaviour using a voltage source, resistors and capacitors [25, 26]. ECMs
have few parameters and can run on a BMS processor, so they dominate on-board use. Their
drawback is that the parameters are empirical and vary with SoC, temperature and age.

2.11.5     Model Parameters of the RC Equivalent Circuit
Adding RC branches captures polarisation. Each element has a physical interpretation:

   • Open-circuit voltage source Voc (z): the equilibrium voltage. Its dependence on z makes
     the model behave like a very large capacitor.

   • Ohmic resistance R0 : electrolyte, foil, tab and contact resistance, responsible for the
     instantaneous step.

   • Polarisation resistance R1 : the voltage drop from charge transfer and diffusion, once
     settled.

   • Polarisation capacitance C1 : sets how quickly that drop develops, with τ1 = R1C1 .

The transient voltage response is governed by these time constants, and the steady-state re-
sponse is reached after about 5τ, when the capacitor current has decayed to zero, and V1 = IR1 .
Thereafter only OCV drifts. Together these yield the dynamic battery behaviour.

2.11.10.5    Genetic Algorithm and Particle Swarm Optimisation
A genetic algorithm evolves a population of parameter sets through selection, crossover and
mutation, using (2.102) as fitness. Particle swarm optimisation moves candidates through the
search space, guided by their own best and the swarm’s best solutions. Both are derivative-free
global methods, useful when the model is nonlinear in its parameters or has many local minima.
They are computationally expensive, so they are used offline.

2.11.10.6    Optimisation-Based Parameter Estimation
Gradient-based nonlinear least squares, as in the MATLAB Parameter Estimator [24], refines
all parameters over drive-cycle data, and is fast when a good initial guess exists. A practical
workflow uses pulse fitting for the initial guess and optimisation for refinement.

2.12        Concept of Cell Balancing
2.12.1      Introduction and Need for Cell Balancing
Cells in a series string carry identical current, and yet they do not stay identical. Since every cell
must remain inside its voltage limits, the pack stops charging when the first cell reaches Vmax ,
and stops discharging when the first reaches Vmin . Cell balancing is the process of equalising
the cells so that the pack can use its full capacity.

2.12.2      Cell Voltage Imbalance, Capacity Imbalance and their Causes
Voltage imbalance denotes a spread of terminal voltages, which, at rest, reflects a spread in
SoC. Capacity imbalance is a spread in the Ah capacity itself. The causes include:

   • manufacturing tolerance in capacity, resistance and self-discharge;

   • temperature gradients across the pack, since warmer cells age and self-discharge faster;

   • unequal ageing and mismatch that grows over the service life;

   • parasitic loads of monitoring electronics that differ between modules.

2.12.6     Cell Balancing Algorithms
2.12.6.1   Voltage-Based and SoC-Based Balancing
Voltage-based balancing equalises measured voltages. It is simple, but voltage under load
includes IR differences, and on flat curves the voltage hardly reveals SoC, so it is normally
done at rest or at the top of charge. SoC-based balancing equalises the estimated SoC and can
run at any time, so it works better for LFP, but it depends on the estimator. Capacity-aware
methods go further by accounting for each cell’s capacity using (2.109).

2.12.6.2   Balancing Thresholds, Current, Losses and Efficiency
The algorithm needs an on-threshold, an off-threshold to prevent chatter, a temperature guard,
and an accurate voltage measurement: thresholds are usually around 5–20 mV, and depend on
the AFE accuracy and chemistry. The balancing charge is proportional to current and time, so
a large pack with low balancing current may need many hours. Balancing loss is the energy not
transferred, and the efficiency of an active balancer is

2.13.8    Communication Interfaces and CAN
Controller Area Network (CAN) is the usual link between BMS and the vehicle. It is a dif-
ferential two-wire, multi-master bus with message priority and error detection. Classic CAN
typically runs at up to 500 kbit/s on such buses, while CAN FD permits larger, faster payloads.
Between AFEs, the common choices are isolated daisy chains and, increasingly, wireless links.

2.13.9    BMS Interaction with EV Control Systems
Figure 2.67 shows the network. The vehicle control unit (VCU) requests a drive or charge
mode. The BMS answers with SoC, power limits and fault status. The inverter respects the
current limits when converting the driver’s torque request. During charging, the BMS talks
to the on-board charger, or through the charge controller to a DC station, and requests target
voltage and current. The thermal system receives coolant requests.

Unit 2 (continued): Battery Chargers and
Charging Systems
The energy that the battery delivers must be returned safely, efficiently and conveniently.
Charging joins three systems that are designed by different parties: the grid, the charging
equipment and the vehicle. Their interaction is governed by power electronics, control,
communication and regulation, in that order of this chapter.


3.1     Basic Requirements for Charging Systems
3.1.1    Introduction and Purpose of EV Battery Chargers
A charger converts electrical power from the supply to the controlled DC voltage and current
demanded by the battery. Its purposes are to restore energy in the required time, without harm-
ing the battery or the grid, and to protect the user. Note a matter of terminology: the equipment
on the wall, the electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE), is a charger only in the DC case. In
AC charging, the EVSE merely delivers protected AC power, while the actual converter is in
the vehicle.

3.1.2    Basic Charging Requirements
   • Charging power, voltage and current. The charger must span the whole battery voltage
     window at the required current, P = Vb Ib . Both output limits matter: for a fixed current
     limit, a low-voltage pack charges at a lower power.

   • Charging efficiency. This is the fraction of grid energy that reaches the battery, including
     AC-DC and DC-DC stages: ηchg = Ebat /Egrid .

   • Charging time. From (3.13) below, time is set by energy and power, and, for chemistry
     reasons, by tapering at high SoC.

   • Battery chemistry compatibility. Voltage limits and permitted rates are chemistry-
     specific, and the charger follows the BMS request.

   • Charging safety and isolation. Grid and battery must be isolated in most designs by a
     high-frequency transformer, so that a single fault cannot put the chassis at a dangerous
     potential.

   • Power factor. The charger must draw near-sinusoidal current in phase with the voltage,
     which requires an active power factor correction stage.

3.2.1.2   Single-Phase, Three-Phase and Bidirectional OBCs
Single-phase OBCs serve domestic connections and are commonly around 3.3 kW or 7.4 kW.
The input power pulsates at twice line frequency, so the DC link needs a large capacitor. Three-

power flow. Bidirectional OBCs replace diodes with active switches, typically using a totem-
pole PFC and DAB, so power can flow from the battery to the AC side.

and connections from the MV level may use a dedicated transformer. The charging cable and
connector are often liquid-cooled at the highest currents, to keep the cable manageable. On the
battery-side interface, the DC is connected straight to the pack through vehicle-side contactors.
The charger controller exchanges limits with the BMS and, separately, connects to a back-end

3.2.3     Other Charging Architectures
3.2.3.1   Conductive Charging
All the above are conductive charging, in which a metallic connection carries power. It is
efficient and simple, and it is the worldwide norm.

3.2.3.2   Wireless Charging: Static and Dynamic
Wireless (inductive) charging transfers power between a ground coil and a vehicle coil through
a resonant, high-frequency magnetic field. Compensation capacitors tune the coils so that a
modest coupling across an air gap can transfer power. Static charging operates in a parking
bay and offers convenience. Dynamic charging supplies power to a moving vehicle via coils
embedded in the road, which might reduce the battery size, but it has high infrastructure cost
and is still at the pilot stage. Efficiency depends strongly on alignment.

Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                   B24EES502


3.2.3.3   Battery Swapping
In battery swapping, a discharged pack is replaced by a charged one in minutes. It separates
charging from the vehicle’s parked time, and is attractive for two- and three-wheeler fleets in
India. Packs may be charged off-peak. It needs standard packs, a stock of extra batteries, and a
business model for ownership. IS 17896 addresses swap systems [30].

3.2.3.4   Bidirectional Charging: V2G, V2H, V2L and V2B
Bidirectional charging treats the vehicle battery as a distributed energy resource:

    • Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G): energy is exported to the grid under aggregator or utility control,
      for peak support or frequency services.

    • Vehicle-to-Home (V2H): the vehicle supplies a household, usually in a backup role, or
      to shift solar energy.

    • Vehicle-to-Building (V2B): the principle of V2H is scaled for a commercial building,
      chiefly to shave peak demand.

    • Vehicle-to-Load (V2L): the vehicle offers an AC socket to power tools or appliances.

The cost of V2G is the extra cycling of the battery, and the need for grid-code compliance,
anti-islanding and metering, together with the high-level communication of ISO 15118-20 [31].

3.3.5    Trickle Charging, Multi-Stage and Pulse Charging
Trickle charging. A small continuous current compensates for self-discharge. It suits lead-
acid and NiMH float service. It is not used in the usual sense for lithium-ion cells, because they
cannot absorb overcharge; however, a small current is used to recover deeply discharged cells
to the safe voltage, which is often called pre-charge or trickle.


Multi-stage charging. A series of decreasing constant currents, sometimes called multi-stage
constant current, is used, with each stage ending at a voltage limit. It approximates the battery’s
permissible current, with a higher rate at low SoC where plating risk is low.


Pulse charging. Current is applied in pulses separated by rests or brief discharges, aiming to
allow concentration gradients to relax. Reported benefits vary [32], so it should be seen as an
option to verify experimentally for each cell.

3.3.6    Fast Charging and Opportunity Charging
Fast charging at rates of about 1–3C or more is limited by lithium plating, heat, and the power
available. Modern protocols use a stored map of allowed current against SoC and temperature
[32]. Opportunity charging is a short top-up whenever the vehicle stops, such as a bus at a
terminal. It keeps the battery at partial SoC, and permits a smaller pack, which suits LFP and
LTO chemistries.

3.4.1     Charging Current Limitations
3.4.1.1   AC Charging Current
The current in AC charging is limited by the lowest of four values: the installation’s circuit
rating, the EVSE rating, the cable rating and the vehicle’s OBC rating. In Mode 3, the EVSE
tells the vehicle its limit through the control pilot, and the OBC must not exceed it. IEC 61851-1
defines charging modes as summarised in Table 3.3 [33].

3.4.1.2   DC Charging Current and Voltage–Current Operating Limits
In DC charging, the station and vehicle exchange their maximum voltage, current and power,
and the vehicle requests a current inside the overlap. The EVSE follows [34]. Its operating
envelope has a constant-current boundary (limited by the cable and module current), a constant-
power boundary (limited by supply), and a maximum voltage boundary. A 400 V car at a
500 A, 1000 V station is therefore limited to about 200 kW by its own voltage, and a station’s
nameplate figure is not the same as the power delivered.

3.4.2     Connector and Cable Ratings
A connector’s rated current depends on its contact design and permitted temperature. Thus the
rating in an IEC configuration is a maximum, while a product may be rated lower. Cables must
be rated for the current at the operating temperature; high-power DC cables use liquid cooling,
and the current is often limited by a temperature sensor at the contacts. IEC 62196-1 (2022
edition) sets general limits up to 690 V AC at 250 A, and 1500 V DC at 800 A [35]. The
specific configurations are covered in Section 3.5.

3.4.3     Electrical Protection
3.4.3.1   Overcurrent Protection
Every charging point needs a dedicated final circuit with its own overcurrent device. The design
load must be taken as continuous, as a charging session runs at full current for hours, and
a diversity factor of unity generally applies unless an automatic load management system is
installed [36, 37].

3.4.3.2   Residual Current Protection
A person’s safety relies upon residual current devices (RCDs). In a charger, the rectifier can
produce smooth DC fault currents, which can blind a conventional Type AC or Type A RCD
by saturating its core. IEC 60364-7-722 therefore requires protection for each connection point
by an RCD with a rated residual current of not more than 30 mA, of at least Type A, with
additional measures against DC fault currents [38]. Measures commonly used are a Type B
RCD, or a Type A RCD together with a residual direct current detecting device (RDC-DD) to
IEC 62955 [39], which disconnects the supply on DC fault current. For Mode 2, IEC 62752
covers the IC-CPD [40]. In India, the National Electrical Code 2023 follows a similar approach
[36], as summarised in [37].

3.4.3.3   Earthing, Grounding and Isolation Monitoring
The protective conductor bonds the vehicle body to the installation earth, and its continuity is
continuously verified via the control pilot circuit. Indian practice refers to IS 3043 for earthing
and IS 732 for wiring [37]. In isolated DC systems, an insulation monitoring device (IMD)

DIN SPEC 70121 preceded ISO 15118, and defines DC charging communication over the

3.5.3.4   OCPP
The Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP), from the Open Charge Alliance, links a charging
station to its central management system, for authorisation, status, metering and remote control.
OCPP 1.6 and 2.0.1 are widely deployed, and OCPP 2.1 has been published as IEC 63584-
210:2025 [55]. OCPI is a separate protocol for roaming among networks.

3.5.3.5   EV–EVSE Communication, Authorisation, Plug and Charge, and Smart Charg-
          ing
A DC session proceeds through a handshake: connection, compatibility check (limits), isolation
test, pre-charge, energy transfer under the vehicle’s current requests, and shut-down. Charging
authorisation may be by RFID, an app, or a payment terminal. Plug and Charge uses digital
certificates under ISO 15118, so that the vehicle identifies itself merely on plugging in. Smart
charging schedules or modulates charging current according to tariff, grid and site constraints,
using either the pilot duty cycle or OCPP profiles.
