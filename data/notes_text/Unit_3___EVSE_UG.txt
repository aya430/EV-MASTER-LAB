An electric vehicle is, electrically, a chain of energy conversions: the grid supplies alternating

from converters (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), through the drive train and its mechanics (Sections 3.3
to 3.6), to the two dominant traction machines, the induction motor (Sections 3.7 to 3.11) and
the permanent magnet synchronous machine (Sections 3.12 to 3.16).

3.1.1    Introduction to Power Electronics
Power electronics is the efficient conversion and control of electrical power using semiconduc-
tor switches. A switch is ideally either fully on, with no voltage across it, or fully off, with no
current through it, so it dissipates no power in either state. Output voltage is then controlled
not by dropping voltage across a resistive element, but by the fraction of time the switch is
on. Inductors and capacitors, which also store energy without dissipation, smooth the chopped
waveform. This is why conversion efficiencies of 95–99% are routinely achieved at tens of
kilowatts [1–3].

3.1.2    Importance of Power Electronic Converters in Electric Vehicles
Converters decide much of what a driver experiences: acceleration comes from fast torque
control, range depends on conversion losses, and charging time depends on charger power [4,5].
Every watt lost in an inverter must also be removed by the cooling system. In summary, the
converters must

   • supply the machine with variable voltage and frequency from a fixed-chemistry DC
     source whose voltage varies with state of charge;

   • permit energy to flow in reverse during braking;

   • interface the vehicle to the grid at near-unity power factor;

   • feed the low-voltage electrical system, the compressor and the heater from the high-
     voltage battery.

3.1.4     Classification of Converters
3.1.4.1   AC-DC Converters
A rectifier converts AC to DC. In the vehicle, it appears in the charger input, and the traction
inverter itself acts as an active rectifier during regeneration.

3.1.4.2   DC-DC Converters
A DC-DC converter changes the level of a DC voltage. Examples include the traction boost
stage, the HV-to-12 V converter and the DC stage of a charger.

School of Electrical and Electronics Engineering                                                                                14
Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                    B24EES502


3.1.4.3   DC-AC Converters
An inverter synthesises AC from DC. The traction inverter is the largest example.

3.1.4.4   DC-AC-DC Conversion
Isolation is not possible at DC, because a transformer needs changing flux. An isolated DC-DC
converter therefore inverts the DC to high-frequency AC, passes it through a small transformer,
and rectifies it again. Since transformer size falls as frequency rises, this DC-AC-DC structure
is far smaller than a line-frequency transformer of the same rating.

3.1.4.5   Unidirectional and Bidirectional Converters
A unidirectional converter passes power one way and can use diodes in place of some switches,
which is simpler and cheaper. A bidirectional converter uses active switches in every position,
so current can reverse. Traction stages must be bidirectional, and chargers are increasingly so.

3.1.4.6   Isolated and Non-Isolated Converters
A non-isolated converter has a conductive path between input and output, and is small and
efficient. An isolated converter has a transformer, which provides safety separation and a large
voltage ratio, at some cost in size and loss. Table 3.2 compares the converters used in an EV.

3.1.6.5   Active Front-End Converters
An active front end (AFE) is a PWM rectifier: a VSI connected to the grid through inductors.
By shaping its AC voltage relative to grid voltage, it controls current magnitude and phase, so
it draws sinusoidal current at any chosen power factor and can return power. It is therefore the
grid-side stage of a bidirectional charger.

3.1.7     Power Electronic Interface Between Battery and Motor
Figure 3.14 shows the battery-to-motor path. The battery, modelled by Vb and Rb , connects

and the link voltage then varies with state of charge and load. The controller sits below the two
power stages and generates all gate signals.

3.1.10     Semiconductor Devices Used in EV Power Electronics
   • Silicon MOSFET. Fast and resistive, used at low voltage up to about 150 V, for example
     in 48 V systems.

   • Silicon IGBT. Bipolar conduction gives a low drop at 600–1200 V and high current.
     Tail current limits switching frequency to around 10–20 kHz. IGBTs have been the
     mainstream traction device.

   • SiC MOSFET. Silicon carbide has a wide bandgap and roughly ten times the critical
     field of silicon, so 1200 V devices have low on-resistance and switch quickly [8]. Lower
     losses, especially at part load, and higher temperature capability make SiC the favoured
     device for 800 V platforms, though at higher cost.

   • GaN HEMT. Very fast and well-suited to on-board chargers and DC-DC converters at
     about 650 V.


3.1.11     Gate Driver Circuits
A gate driver turns a logic-level signal into the gate voltage and charge the device needs. For
a MOSFET or IGBT, the driver charges the gate capacitance through a resistor, which sets
di/dt and dv/dt. Upper-leg drivers need isolated supplies and isolated signal paths, because
the source of the upper device swings at the full link voltage. Drivers also provide dead-time
insertion, desaturation (short-circuit) detection, soft turn-off, undervoltage lockout and, for SiC,
a negative turn-off voltage that resists false turn-on.

3.1.12     DC-Link Capacitor
The DC-link capacitor supplies the pulsed inverter current, keeps the link stiff and absorbs
regenerative transients. Its current rating, not its capacitance, is usually the design constraint:
for a two-level VSI the rms capacitor ripple current is of the order of half the load phase rms
current at the worst-case modulation [6]. A simple sizing rule follows from the charge supplied
in a period, C ≥ Iripple /(2π f s ∆V) as an approximation. Film capacitors are preferred over

inductance must be small, since it causes voltage overshoot when devices switch off.

3.1.13     Thermal Management of Power Converters
Heat flows from the semiconductor junction through a chain of thermal resistances to the
coolant, and the steady-state junction temperature is

                                     T j = T cool + Ploss Rth, jc .                                 (3.18)

Rth, jc (in K/W) here is the total junction-to-coolant value. Traction modules typically use direct
liquid cooling through pin-fin baseplates, on a water-glycol loop that often shares a circuit with
the motor. Temperature cycling fatigues bond wires and solder, so lifetime depends on the
amplitude of temperature swings as well as their peak value. Control software estimates T j and
derates current as limits are approached.

3.2.1     Introduction to EV Charger Topologies

3.2.7     Power Factor and Harmonic Considerations
Distorted current heats transformers and neutral conductors and disturbs other equipment.
Modern PFC stages reach PF above 0.99 and current THD below 5% near rated power. Per-
formance degrades at light load, where ripple current is large relative to the fundamental. EMI
filters are also needed, since switching at tens to hundreds of kilohertz generates high-frequency
conducted noise.

3.2.8     Galvanic Isolation and High-Frequency Transformers
Galvanic isolation means that no conductive path exists between circuits. It prevents a single
insulation fault from connecting the grid to touchable parts. The transformer core area follows
from Faraday’s law. For a square wave,

3.3      Electric Drive Train Overview
3.3.1     Introduction to Electric Drive Trains
The drive train is everything that carries energy from the battery to the road. It splits naturally
into an electrical part and a mechanical part, joined at the motor. An EV drive train is much
simpler than that of a conventional car, since a traction motor produces torque from zero speed
over a wide speed range, so there is no clutch and usually only one gear ratio [15].

3.3.2     EV Powertrain Architecture and Components
Figure 3.32 shows the complete architecture. Heavy double arrows form the energy path, and
dashed arrows are signals. The driver’s pedal request reaches the vehicle control unit, which

Electric Vehicle System Engineering                                                                         B24EES502


issues a torque command to the motor controller after considering limits from the battery man-
agement system. The components are:

    • Battery system. Stores energy and sets the DC voltage, as covered in Unit 2.

    • Power electronic converter. Controls voltage, current and frequency (Section 3.1).

    • Electric traction motor. Converts electrical to mechanical power and back.

    • Transmission system. A reduction gear trades speed for torque, so a small high-speed
      motor can provide high wheel torque.

    • Differential. Divides torque equally between wheels of an axle, while permitting differ-

    • Wheels and tyres. Convert torque to tractive force through the tyre-road contact. Adhe-

    • Motor controller. Executes the fast current loops and generates inverter switching com-
      mands.

    • Vehicle control unit (VCU). Interprets driver demand, arbitrates limits, blends regener-

3.5     Types of Loads
3.5.1    Introduction to Mechanical Loads
A drive is selected by matching the torque-speed capability of the motor to the torque-speed

3.6     Four Quadrant Operation
3.6.1    Introduction
A drive that can produce torque in either direction at speed in either direction has four possible

3.7.5    Torque Production
The rotating field cuts the rotor bars and induces voltages in them. As the cage is short-
circuited, currents flow and these interact with the field to give force on each bar. By Lenz’s
law, the force acts to reduce the relative motion, so it drags the rotor along behind the field. If
the rotor reached synchronous speed, the relative motion would vanish, as would the induced
current and the torque; hence a motor always runs with some slip. Above synchronous speed,
slip is negative, and the machine generates: this is how an induction machine regenerates.
Torque is produced by the interaction of the field with the in-phase component of rotor current,

3.9      Direct and Indirect Vector Control
3.9.1     Introduction to Vector Control
A separately excited DC motor is easy to control because its flux and torque are set indepen-
dently: field current gives flux, armature current gives torque, and the two are perpendicular
by commutator action. In an induction motor, one set of stator currents produces both flux and
torque, so a sudden change in voltage disturbs both. Vector control, based on the principle of
field orientation due to Blaschke [21], restores the DC-machine behaviour by viewing the stator
current in a frame that rotates with the rotor flux.

3.9.3     Need for Decoupled Flux and Torque Control
Three fluxes appear in the machine, and any can be chosen for orientation.

   • Air-gap flux ⃗λm = Lm (⃗i s + ⃗i′r ) links both stator and rotor.

   • Stator flux ⃗λ s = ⃗λm + Lls⃗i s adds stator leakage.

   • Rotor flux ⃗λr = ⃗λm + Llr′ ⃗i′r adds rotor leakage.

Rotor flux orientation gives complete decoupling and is the choice in this unit. Stator and
air-gap orientation retain a coupling term which must be compensated [16, 17]. Torque is pro-
duced by the component of stator current perpendicular to rotor flux. Vector control therefore
keeps the flux component constant and varies the perpendicular component to change torque
immediately.

3.9.7.4    Advantages, Limitations and Rotor Time Constant Dependence
Algorithm 9 is simple and has good low-speed behaviour, which explains its popularity for
traction. Its weakness lies in (3.81), which depends on τr = Lr /Rr . As rotor bars warm by

error in τr gives a wrong slip, the frame then misaligns with the actual flux, and both flux and
torque deviate from their commands. On-line estimation or a thermal model is used to update
Rr . Table 3.14 compares the two.

3.10.10         FOC Operation During Motoring and Regenerative Braking
In motoring, T e∗ > 0, so i∗qs > 0 and ω sl > 0: the flux leads the rotor. In braking at forward
speed, T e∗ < 0, so i∗qs < 0 and ω sl < 0. The frame then runs slower than the rotor, slip is negative
and power reverses, with no change in control structure. This seamless transition is one of the
main benefits of FOC.

3.10.11         Dynamic Response
Figures 3.83 to 3.86 show representative responses, obtained from a simplified simulation with
the torque loop modelled as a 2 ms lag, J = 0.25 kg m2 and a torque limit of 200 N m. A speed
step at t = 0 makes the speed PI saturate at maximum torque, and speed rises as a ramp. At
t = 0.8 s, a load step causes a small dip, which the integrator removes. At t = 1.4 s, a reduced
speed command gives negative torque, and the drive regenerates. Torque follows iqs almost
instantly, because flux is held constant by ids . In contrast, flux build-up after excitation takes
several τr (Figure 3.86). This is why the machine is pre-fluxed before launch, and why iqs , not
ids , is the fast control channel.

3.11.8     Effect of Leakage Inductance
    • Transient response. Fast current changes are limited by σL s , not L s . Stator and rotor
      time constants of the R-L type are therefore much shorter than τr .

    • Current dynamics. Larger leakage lessens PWM ripple, ∆i ∝ Vdc /(σL s f s ), but demands
      more voltage for a given di/dt.

    • Torque production. Leakage reduces breakdown torque, since in (3.65) it appears in the
      denominator. It also caps the constant power speed range.

    • Control performance. Decoupling terms in (3.95) use σL s , and the current-loop gain is
      set by it. An error in leakage shifts the estimated flux angle in (3.77).

the most widely used EV motor [15, 18].

3.12.6     EV Traction Requirements, Advantages and Limitations
Advantages:

   • High power density. Magnets supply flux at no cost in copper, so the machine is com-
     pact.

   • High efficiency. Rotor loss is small, and peak efficiency often reaches around 97%.

   • Regenerative braking. Back EMF is available at all speeds, so generation requires no
     magnetising power.

   • Good thermal path. Most losses are in the stator, near the cooling jacket.

Limitations:

   • Thermal limitations. Remanence falls with temperature, by about 0.1%/K for NdFeB,
     which reduces torque.

   • Demagnetization. Excessive negative d-axis MMF at high temperature can permanently
     damage magnets. Control limits and thermal protection must prevent this.

   • Field weakening. The magnet flux cannot be turned off, so weakening requires continu-
     ous stator current. At high speed, an inverter fault can result in uncontrolled generation
     into the DC bus, because the back EMF can exceed the link voltage.

   • Cost and supply. Rare-earth prices and supply are a strategic concern.

3.15.6       Maximum Torque per Voltage
At high speed in a machine with Ich < Imax , the current circle no longer limits. The largest

current is offset by saliency, and it is usually read from a table [26]. The example machines
have Ich near or above Imax , so MTPV is not reached within their speed range.

3.16.2     Principle of Rotor Flux Orientation
The Park angle is θe = pθm + θ0 , where θ0 is a calibrated offset between the sensor and the
magnet north axis. With this, the d axis lies on the magnet by construction, iq is the torque
current, and id controls the net d-axis flux. Current decoupling then means that, in the rotor
frame, the two currents can be set independently by separate loops.

3.16.3     Rotor Position Feedback
Figure 3.117 shows the position chain. A resolver, a rotary transformer, gives sine and cosine

the automotive standard. A resolver-to-digital converter (RDC) or software tracking observer
extracts angle and speed. An encoder gives higher resolution by digital pulses but is less toler-
ant of the environment. An angle error ∆θ rotates the frame, producing a torque error of about
T e (1 − cos ∆θ) together with d-axis disturbance. Offset calibration is therefore a production

3.16.8        Control of SPMSM and IPMSM
The two machines share one structure, but differ in the reference block. An SPMSM uses
id = 0 and a linear torque constant, with negative id only for field weakening. An IPMSM uses

3.16.11     Motoring, Regeneration and Dynamic Performance
Motoring is i∗q > 0 at forward speed, in which case the voltage and current give Pe > 0. For
regeneration, i∗q < 0. By (3.137), torque changes sign, and the DC current reverses through
the inverter diodes and switches, with no change of control structure. For an IPMSM in regen-
eration, MTPA keeps id < 0 and β moves into the third quadrant of the current plane, since
reluctance torque follows the sign of id iq .

A well-tuned PMSM drive gives a torque rise time of 1–2 ms, far quicker than the mechanical
time constants of a vehicle, so the torque response is effectively instantaneous from the driver’s
viewpoint. Performance is constrained not by the controller but by DC-link voltage (particu-
larly at low state of charge), by thermal limits, and by torsional oscillation in the driveline, for
which the rate limiter of Algorithm 19 and active damping are used. In MATLAB/Simulink,
the same structure can be assembled with Simscape Electrical and Motor Control Blockset
